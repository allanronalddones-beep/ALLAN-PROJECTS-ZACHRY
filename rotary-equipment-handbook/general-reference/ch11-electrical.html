The Philippines runs at 60 Hz. A 4-pole motor turns 1800 rpm synchronous, roughly 1770 rpm loaded. Equipment and curves from 50 Hz practice must be corrected — see the affinity-law consequences in §4.3. A 50 Hz-wound motor energised at 60 Hz will also be over-fluxed or under-torqued depending on whether the voltage is scaled with frequency; check the nameplate for a dual rating before assuming it is usable.

A shaft-mounted fan stops cooling when the motor slows. An IC 411 (TEFC) motor on a VFD loses cooling capacity roughly in proportion to speed while still carrying torque. Below about 50 % speed at full torque it overheats. Constant-torque variable-speed duty requires IC 416 — a separately driven, constant-speed blower. This is one of the most common and most expensive VFD retrofit mistakes.

Vertical motors carry the pump thrust. An IM V1 motor for a vertical pump needs a thrust bearing rated for the hydraulic down-thrust plus rotor weight, and often a non-reverse ratchet to stop backward rotation when the pump is shut down against a full column. Both are purchase-order items, not standard features.

11.1.4Insulation and temperature rise

Table 11.1-5 — Insulation classes and permitted rise (40 °C ambient, measured by resistance, service factor 1.0)
ClassMax. hot-spot temperaturePermitted riseHot-spot allowance
A105 °C60 K5 K
E120 °C75 K5 K
B130 °C80 K10 K
F155 °C105 K10 K
H180 °C125 K15 K
N200 °C——
"Class F insulation with Class B rise" is the standard industrial specification, and it is not redundancy for its own sake. The motor is built with Class F material (155 °C capability) but guaranteed not to exceed Class B rise (80 K). That 25 K margin roughly quadruples insulation life — as a rule of thumb, insulation life halves for every 10 K of continuous over-temperature. It also absorbs voltage unbalance, high ambient and harmonic heating. Specify it.
Ambient above 40 °C must be stated on the datasheet. The rise limits above assume a 40 °C ambient. A motor in a Philippine plant room at 50 °C has 10 K less headroom and must be derated or specified for the higher ambient. Vendors do not assume a tropical ambient unless told.

Table 11.1-6 — IEC 60034-30-1 efficiency classes

Efficiency is a lifecycle argument, not a purchase-price argument. A continuously running motor consumes its purchase price in electricity within months. Two efficiency points on a 500 kW motor running 8000 h/year is roughly 80 MWh per year. The payback on the premium is normally measured in months.

11.1.6Starting

Table 11.1-7 — Starting methods
MethodStarting currentStarting torqueNotes
Direct on line (DOL)6 – 8 × FLC100 %Simplest and cheapest. Limited by supply capacity and voltage dip.
Star–delta⅓ of DOL⅓ of DOLCheap reduced-voltage start. Transition transient can be severe. Only for low starting-torque loads.
Autotransformer(tap)² × DOL(tap)² × DOLSelectable taps — typically 50, 65, 80 %. Better torque per ampere than star–delta.
Soft starter2 – 4 × FLCReduced, rampedSmooth, adjustable, controls mechanical shock. No speed control once running.
Variable frequency drive≈ 1 × FLCUp to 150 %+Full torque at low current, plus speed control. Most expensive; brings harmonics and bearing-current issues.
ReactorReducedReduced as current²Simple, robust, used on large MV motors
Torque falls as the square of voltage. Any reduced-voltage start reduces torque by the square of the voltage ratio. A star–delta start gives one third of the torque. If the load needs more than that to accelerate — a loaded screw compressor, a high-inertia fan — the motor will stall at part speed, draw locked-rotor current and trip or burn. Check the load torque-speed curve against the motor's reduced-voltage curve, with the actual voltage dip, before selecting the method.
Table 11.1-8 — NEMA MG 1 design letters

Voltage drop on starting

Rule of thumb. Starting voltage dip at the motor terminals is normally limited to about 15 % (85 % retained), and the dip at the common busbar to about 10 %, so that other running motors are not dragged down and contactors do not drop out. A motor that is large relative to the supply transformer will violate this on DOL and forces a reduced-voltage method. The calculation needs the source impedance, so it belongs to the electrical engineer — but the mechanical engineer should raise it when the motor exceeds roughly 15–20 % of the transformer rating.

Specify bearing insulation on VFD-fed motors. A VFD's fast switching produces common-mode voltage that discharges through the bearings, pitting and fluting the raceways and destroying them in months. The standard countermeasures are an insulated non-drive-end bearing, a shaft grounding ring, and output filtering. None of these are fitted unless specified, and the failure looks like a lubrication problem, so it is often misdiagnosed and repeated.

11.4Hazardous area classification

Zones and Divisions

Table 11.4-1 — IEC/ATEX Zones against NEC Divisions
IEC Zone (gas)DefinitionNEC Division
Zone 0Explosive atmosphere present continuously, or for long periods, or frequentlyDivision 1
Zone 1Likely to occur in normal operation occasionally
Zone 2Not likely in normal operation, and if it does occur, only brieflyDivision 2
The three-zone system is finer than the two-division system. Division 1 covers both Zone 0 and Zone 1. A conversion from Zone to Division is therefore conservative and straightforward; converting Division 1 to a Zone requires a judgement about which zone actually applies. Dust has a parallel scheme: Zones 20, 21, 22 against Class II Divisions 1 and 2.

Gas groups — the lettering trap

Table 11.4-2 — Gas groups, IEC against NEC

The letters run in opposite directions. IEC IIC is the most hazardous group; NEC Group C is intermediate. IEC IIA is the least hazardous; NEC Group A is the most. Reading "Group C" on a nameplate and assuming it covers hydrogen because IIC does is a genuine and dangerous error. Always check which scheme the nameplate is using — the IEC form is written "II C", with the Roman numeral.

Table 11.4-4 — Temperature classes (IEC and NEC share the T numbering)
ClassMax. surface temperatureExample gases requiring it or better

Lower T number means a hotter permitted surface. Equipment marked T4 is suitable wherever T1, T2 or T3 is required, because its surface stays cooler. The rule is: the equipment's T class number must be equal to or higher than the number demanded by the gas. T4 equipment satisfies a T3 requirement; T3 equipment does not satisfy T4.
Equipment Protection Levels. Modern IEC practice adds an EPL suffix expressing the protection afforded: Ga, Gb, Gc for gas and Da, Db, Dc for dust. Zone 0 needs Ga, Zone 1 needs Gb or better, Zone 2 needs Gc or better. The EPL has largely replaced reasoning directly from the zone when selecting equipment.

The tE time on an Ex e motor is a protection setting, not a data-sheet curiosity. tE is the time the stalled motor takes to reach its limiting temperature from rated running temperature. The overload protection must trip within tE at locked-rotor current, and the relay must be certified for the duty. An Ex e motor with a conventional thermal overload set by rule of thumb is not protected.
Never open an Ex d enclosure in a live hazardous area, and never damage a flame path. An Ex d enclosure's safety depends entirely on the gap and length of its machined flame paths. Scratching a flange face, substituting a bolt with one of lower grade or shorter engagement, adding paint to the joint, or using the wrong gasket all defeat the protection. Flame-path faces are protected with a thin smear of approved grease, never sealant.

The conversion only runs one way. A NEMA type can be said to meet or exceed the corresponding IP rating. The reverse is not true. NEMA 250 tests things the IP code does not address at all — corrosion resistance, the effect of external ice formation, oil and coolant exposure, and gasket ageing. An enclosure marked IP66 has not been shown to meet Type 4X, and substituting one for the other on a coastal site is how stainless requirements get quietly lost.
