13.1Installation flow

Table 13.1-1 — Sequence from delivery to handover
#StepKey points and hold points
1Receipt and inspectionCheck against packing list; inspect for transit damage before signing; verify shock/tilt indicators; confirm preservation intact
2Storage and preservationIndoor where specified; nitrogen blanket maintained; shafts rotated on a schedule; desiccant replaced; records kept
3Foundation acceptanceConcrete strength and cure verified; anchor bolt positions and projections checked; surface chipped to sound aggregate. Hold point.
4Baseplate settingSet on jackscrews or shims; level within tolerance in two axes; anchor bolts fitted but not finally tightened
5GroutingForms sealed, grout placed to avoid voids, cured per the manufacturer. Hold point.
6Anchor bolt tighteningAfter grout has cured, to the specified torque or tension, in sequence
7Soft foot check and correctionBefore any alignment. Hold point.
8Preliminary alignmentCold, with thermal offsets applied
9Piping connectionFree and unstrained — the pipe must come to the nozzle, not be pulled to it. Hold point.
10Pipe strain checkRe-check alignment with piping bolted and unbolted; movement beyond tolerance means the pipe must be re-worked
11Final alignmentRecorded and signed. Hold point.
12Coupling installationHub advance recorded; match marks aligned; spacer fitted; guard installed
13Auxiliary systemsLube oil flushing to the specified cleanliness; seal systems commissioned; cooling water proven
14Pre-commissioning checksRotation direction, protection function tests, instrument calibration, interlock proving
15Solo run and performance runDriver solo first, then coupled. Vibration and bearing temperature recorded.
16Hot alignment checkAfter thermal stabilisation on critical machines
Pipe strain is the most commonly missed step and one of the most damaging. Piping forced into place pulls the casing out of alignment, distorts it, and loads the bearings continuously. Check alignment with the flange bolts loose and again with them tight: movement beyond the alignment tolerance means the pipe is pulling and must be cut and re-fitted. API 686 and API 610 both set nozzle load limits; exceeding them voids the vendor's alignment and vibration guarantees.

Table 13.2-2 — Grout selection
CementitiousEpoxy

Epoxy grout expands when it cures and when it gets hot. Large epoxy pours generate an exotherm and have a much higher coefficient of thermal expansion than concrete. Place in lifts, respect the maximum pour depth, and provide expansion joints in long pours. Ignoring this cracks the grout or the foundation, and a cracked grout bed is a complete re-do.
Voids under the baseplate defeat everything else. Grout must fill completely. Vent holes and grout holes in the baseplate, a head box to maintain hydrostatic pressure, and placing from one side to push air ahead of the flow are the standard means. Hammer-test the baseplate after cure — a hollow sound means a void, and a void means the baseplate is not supported where the calculation assumed it was.

13.3Balancing

Balance quality grades

ISO 21940-11 (which superseded ISO 1940-1) defines balance quality grade G as the product of permissible specific unbalance and angular velocity:

G= eper⁢ω eper= 9549⁢G n
G
balance quality grade, mm/s
eper
permissible residual specific unbalance, g·mm/kg (numerically equal to µm of eccentricity)
n
maximum service speed, rpm

The permissible residual unbalance for the rotor is then:

Uper= eper⁢m
Uper
permissible residual unbalance, g·mm
m
rotor mass, kg
Table 13.3-1 — Balance quality grades for rotating equipment
GradeTypical rotors
G 0.4Precision grinder spindles, gyroscopes
G 1Precision machine tool drives, small high-speed armatures
G 2.5Gas and steam turbines, turbo-compressors, turbo-generators, machine tool drives, medium and large electric armatures, pumps with turbine drive. The usual API machinery grade.
G 6.3Pump impellers, fans, flywheels, centrifuge drums, general machine parts, standard electric motor armatures
G 16Drive shafts, parts of agricultural machinery, crankshaft components of multi-cylinder engines
G 40Car wheels, drive shafts of elastically mounted engines
G 100 and aboveReciprocating engine components; coarser classes

Worked example. A 180 kg compressor rotor running at 11 000 rpm, specified to G 2.5.

eper = 9549 × 2.5 / 11 000 = 2.17 g·mm/kg

Uper = 2.17 × 180 = 391 g·mm total, normally divided between two correction planes — roughly 195 g·mm per plane. That is a 0.2 g mass at 1 m radius, or 20 g at 10 mm radius: a smear of dirt on an impeller matters at this speed.

Higher speed means a tighter tolerance for the same grade. eper is inversely proportional to speed. The same G 2.5 grade allows 24 g·mm/kg at 1000 rpm but only 2.4 g·mm/kg at 10 000 rpm. This is why high-speed rotors must be kept scrupulously clean, why coupling match marks matter, and why a single lost balance weight is a trip-level event.
Table 13.3-2 — Single-plane, two-plane and at-speed balancing
MethodWhen
Single plane (static)Disc-shaped rotors where the diameter is large relative to the length — a single fan or impeller
Two plane (dynamic)Rotors whose length is significant relative to diameter. The normal case.

Table 13.4-2 — What the zones mean

DSevere enough to cause damage. Investigate and correct.
Rigid or flexible support — the definition that decides which row applies. The support is rigid if the first natural frequency of the machine-support system in the measurement direction is above the main excitation frequency (usually running speed), typically by at least 25 %. Otherwise it is flexible. A large machine on a massive block foundation is usually rigid; the same machine on a steel structure or skid is usually flexible. Getting this wrong moves the acceptance limit by a factor of about 1.5.
Broadband velocity is a screening measure, not a diagnosis. A machine can sit comfortably in Zone B while a bearing fails, because early rolling-element bearing defects appear at high frequency where velocity amplitude is small. Overall velocity catches unbalance, misalignment and looseness. Bearing condition needs envelope/demodulated acceleration or shock pulse measurement. For machines with fluid-film bearings, API 670 shaft-relative proximity probes measure what actually matters — shaft movement within the clearance.

13.5Shaft alignment

Soft foot — always first

Correct soft foot before attempting alignment. Soft foot is any condition where the machine feet do not sit flat on the baseplate, so that tightening a hold-down bolt distorts the casing. Alignment carried out over an uncorrected soft foot cannot be made to repeat, and the distortion loads the bearings continuously. Check by loosening each foot bolt in turn with a dial indicator on that foot; movement beyond about 0.05 mm requires shimming. Note that angular soft foot (a foot that is not parallel to the base) cannot be fixed with flat shims and needs machining.

Methods

Table 13.5-1 — Alignment methods
MethodAccuracyNotes
Straightedge and feeler gaugeVery lowRough positioning only. Not an alignment method for any coupled machine.
Rim and face (dial indicators)ModerateNeeds a face reading; affected by axial float. Works for short couplings.
Reverse dial indicatorGoodTwo rim readings, no face reading. Unaffected by axial float. The classic method for spacer couplings.
Laser alignmentHighestFast, computes moves directly, records results. Standard practice now.
Bar sag must be measured, not estimated. With dial indicator methods, the bracket holding the indicator sags under its own weight, and that sag appears in the readings as a real misalignment. Measure it by mounting the bracket on a rigid pipe, rotating 180°, and recording the change. Subtract it from the field readings. Laser systems remove this error entirely, which is a large part of why they displaced dial indicators.

Published alignment tolerance tables differ between sources, and API 686 and individual machine vendors set their own requirements. Use the table above for planning and expectation-setting, and the specified figures for acceptance. Record the achieved values either way.

Thermal offset

The machines must be aligned cold such that they come into alignment hot. Use the thermal growth relation from §3.3:

ΔL= α⁢L⁢ΔT

where L is the vertical distance from the foot/shim plane to the shaft centreline for each machine, and ΔT is the rise of that supporting metal — not the process temperature. Growth of the pump and growth of the driver partly cancel; only the difference is set as the cold offset.

Get the offsets from the vendor where they exist. Vendors of hot machines publish recommended cold alignment offsets that account for casing, pedestal and baseplate growth together. These are more reliable than a hand calculation. Where none exist, measure hot alignment on the first unit and correct the rest.

13.6Bolt tightening

Torque and preload

T= K⁢F⁢d
T
applied torque, N·m
K
nut factor — dimensionless, dominated by friction
F
bolt preload (tension), N
d
nominal bolt diameter, m
Table 13.6-1 — Indicative nut factor K
ConditionK
Dry, as-received steel0.20 – 0.25
Machine oil0.18 – 0.21
General purpose grease0.15 – 0.18
Molybdenum disulfide / nickel anti-seize0.10 – 0.15
PTFE-based compound0.08 – 0.12
Torque control is a poor way to achieve a known preload. Roughly 90 % of applied torque is consumed by friction under the nut face and in the threads, and only about 10 % becomes bolt tension. Because K varies by a factor of two or more with lubrication, surface condition and reuse, torque control delivers a preload scatter commonly quoted at ±25 to ±30 %. Where preload matters — flange joints, high-pressure casings, critical hold-downs — use bolt tensioning, turn-of-nut, or direct bolt-elongation measurement instead.
Specify the lubricant with the torque. A torque figure without a stated lubrication condition is incomplete. Applying a dry-assembly torque to a moly-lubricated stud can overload and yield it; applying a lubricated torque to a dry stud leaves the joint loose. The lubricant is part of the specification.

Flange joint assembly

Table 13.6-2 — ASME PCC-1 assembly sequence, in outline
PassTargetPattern
Hand tight / snugContact, flange faces parallelCheck gap at four points

Why the final rotational pass matters. Tightening any bolt relaxes its neighbours — elastic interaction. A cross pattern alone leaves a very uneven load distribution around the flange. The final pass round the circle equalises it, and is what actually produces a leak-free joint. PCC-1 also gives alternative patterns that reach uniform load in fewer passes for large-diameter flanges.
