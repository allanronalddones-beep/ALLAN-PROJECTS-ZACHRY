14  Operation

Starting, running and lubricating the machine — the checks that prevent most failures.

This chapter is generic guidance, not an operating procedure. Every machine has a vendor operating manual and every plant has approved procedures and permits. Those govern. Use this chapter to understand why a step exists and to check that nothing obvious is missing from a local procedure — never in place of one.

14.1Centrifugal pump operation

14.1.1Checks before start-up

Table 14.1-1 — Pre-start checks
AreaCheck
Permits and isolationWork permits closed; electrical isolation removed; all personnel clear; coupling guard fitted
LubricationOil level at the mark in the bearing housing; constant level oiler charged and the bottle visibly holding oil; correct grade confirmed; grease points attended if grease lubricated
CoolingBearing and seal cooling water lined up and flowing; jacket vents clear
Seal systemFlush plan lined up; barrier/buffer fluid at correct level and pressure; quench and drain lined up; seal pot pressure above stuffing box pressure for a Plan 53
SuctionSuction valve fully open; strainer condition known and differential monitored; suction vessel level above minimum
Priming and ventingCasing fully primed and vented — all air displaced. For self-priming or vertical can pumps, follow the specific procedure.
DischargeDischarge valve closed or cracked open, per the pump's specific speed (see below); minimum flow bypass open; non-return valve free
MechanicalShaft turns freely by hand; direction of rotation confirmed; coupling in good condition; hold-down bolts tight
Instruments and protectionGauges valved in; vibration and temperature monitoring live; low-flow and low-level trips proven
Warm-up (hot service)Pump warmed through at the specified rate before start; casing drain used for circulation; standby pumps kept warm
Never start a centrifugal pump dry. The mechanical seal faces are lubricated by the pumped liquid and run dry for only seconds before they are destroyed. Priming and venting is not a formality — it is the single most important pre-start step, and it must be repeated after any maintenance that opened the casing.

14.1.2Start-up procedure

  1. Confirm the pre-start checks are complete and the suction valve is fully open.
  2. Set the discharge valve: closed or cracked open for a radial (low specific speed) pump, to limit starting power; open for an axial or high specific speed mixed-flow pump, which draws maximum power at shutoff.
  3. Start the driver. Confirm it comes up to speed promptly and the ammeter settles.
  4. Confirm discharge pressure develops. If it does not within a few seconds, stop and re-prime.
  5. Open the discharge valve slowly to the required duty, watching discharge pressure and motor current.
  6. Confirm flow is above the minimum continuous stable flow before closing the bypass.
  7. Check the seal — no visible leakage, barrier fluid pressure and level steady.
  8. Record vibration, bearing temperatures, suction and discharge pressure, and motor current once conditions settle.
Do not leave a pump running against a closed discharge. All absorbed power becomes heat in the trapped liquid. Temperature rise can be rapid — the relation is in §4.5 — and the consequences run from seal failure to flashing and casing damage. Time at shutoff must be measured in seconds, not minutes, and a minimum flow bypass must be in service.

14.1.3Checks during operation

Table 14.1-2 — Routine operating checks
ParameterWhat to look for
Suction and discharge pressureDifferential consistent with the curve at the current flow. A falling differential suggests wear ring clearance, impeller damage or gas entrainment.
FlowWithin the preferred operating region; above minimum continuous stable flow
Motor currentWithin nameplate; a rise at constant duty points to mechanical drag or a density change
Bearing temperatureStable. Oil-lubricated sleeve bearings typically run well below 80 °C; rolling-element housings are commonly limited around 80–90 °C. Compare against the baseline for that machine.
VibrationAgainst the ISO 20816 zones in §13.4 and against this machine's own baseline. A change matters more than an absolute number.
SealNo visible leakage; barrier fluid level stable — a falling seal pot level means the inner seal is leaking
NoiseA gravelly, rattling sound is cavitation. A rhythmic knock suggests looseness or a mechanical fault.
Oil level and conditionAt the mark; clear, not cloudy. Cloudy oil means water ingress.
Cooling waterFlowing, with a reasonable temperature rise. No flow means no indication unless there is a flow sight.
LeaksCasing, flanges, auxiliary piping, drains
Trend, do not just read. An absolute value within limits tells you little. A bearing that has been running at 62 °C for two years and is now at 71 °C is telling you something important even though it is well inside its limit. Baseline every machine after commissioning and compare against the baseline.

14.1.4Shutdown

  1. Reduce flow by closing the discharge valve partially — not below minimum continuous flow.
  2. Open the minimum flow bypass if it is not already in service.
  3. Stop the driver.
  4. Close the discharge valve fully once the pump has stopped, to prevent reverse rotation if the non-return valve passes.
  5. Leave the suction valve open and the pump full, unless the pump is being drained for maintenance.
  6. Keep seal barrier systems and, for hot service, warm-up circulation in service so the pump stays ready and does not thermally shock on restart.
  7. For extended shutdown, apply the preservation regime — rotate the shaft periodically, maintain a nitrogen blanket where specified.
Reverse rotation can unscrew an impeller. If the discharge non-return valve passes and the system drains back through a stopped pump, the pump runs backwards as a turbine. On pumps with threaded impeller retention this can unscrew the impeller, and it can overspeed. Close the discharge valve after stopping, and verify non-return valves on vertical and high-static-head services.

14.1.5Troubleshooting

Table 14.1-3 — Common symptoms and likely causes
SymptomLikely causes, most common first
No flow at startNot primed; suction valve shut; wrong rotation; suction strainer blocked; air lock in the suction line
Insufficient flow or headRunning off-curve; worn wear rings; impeller damage or fouling; entrained gas; wrong speed; viscosity higher than design
Flow falls off after a periodSuction strainer fouling; vessel level falling; vapour binding; wear
Cavitation noise and erratic pressureNPSHa below NPSHr — suction level low, suction line fouled, liquid hotter than design, suction pressure fallen, flow beyond run-out
High vibrationOperating below minimum flow; misalignment; unbalance from wear or deposits; cavitation; bearing damage; pipe strain; resonance
High bearing temperatureOver-filled or under-filled oil; wrong oil grade; cooling water lost; bearing damage; excessive thrust from wear ring clearance; misalignment
Seal leaks shortly after overhaulDry run at start; faces damaged in assembly; wrong setting dimension; flush plan not lined up; excessive shaft deflection from off-curve operation
Motor overloadFlow beyond run-out; SG or viscosity above design; mechanical drag; low voltage; wrong impeller fitted
Repeated bearing failuresMisalignment; pipe strain; contamination; wrong lubricant; operating off BEP; vibration from an unresolved cause
Most "pump problems" are system problems. Low flow, cavitation, high vibration and short seal life usually trace back to the pump being operated away from the duty it was bought for — because the system resistance is not what was calculated, or the pump was oversized at the design stage. Before overhauling, establish where on its curve the pump is actually running.

14.2General purpose steam turbine operation

Before start-up

Table 14.2-1 — Steam turbine pre-start checks
AreaCheck
DrainsAll casing, steam chest and line drains open. Condensate must be removed before steam reaches the blading.
LubricationOil level correct; auxiliary oil pump running and pressure established; oil temperature acceptable; filters clean
Governor and tripGovernor reset; trip and throttle valve reset and free; overspeed trip tested within its interval
Steam qualityInlet steam at specified pressure and superheat. Wet steam erodes blading.
ExhaustBack pressure line open, or condenser vacuum established and gland sealing steam on for a condensing machine
CoolingOil cooler and gland condenser cooling water flowing
MechanicalRotor turns freely; coupling guard fitted; casing supports free to slide on their keys
Driven machineReady to accept drive and correctly lined up for start

Start-up

  1. Confirm all drains are open and establish lube oil pressure.
  2. Warm the casing through slowly with steam admitted at a low rate, following the vendor's ramp. Watch casing and shaft differential expansion where instrumented.
  3. Roll the rotor and raise speed slowly, passing through any critical speeds without dwelling.
  4. Hold at the specified warm-up speed until temperatures stabilise and vibration settles.
  5. Close drains progressively as condensate clears.
  6. Raise to governor control speed and verify the governor takes control.
  7. Test the overspeed trip if the procedure requires it on this start.
  8. Load gradually. Confirm the auxiliary oil pump cuts out and the shaft-driven pump holds pressure.
  9. Record vibration, bearing temperatures, steam conditions and speed.
Water in a turbine is catastrophic and fast. A slug of condensate reaching the blading can bend or break blades and bow the rotor in seconds. Drains open on start-up, proper warm-through, functioning steam traps, and attention to inlet steam quality are what prevent it. Never rush a cold start to save time.
Do not dwell at a critical speed. Pass through critical speeds promptly and smoothly. Holding there builds up amplitude and can rub the seals or damage bearings. The critical speeds are in the vendor's documentation and should be marked on the local speed indicator.

During operation and shutdown

Table 14.2-2 — Running checks and shutdown notes
ItemNote
Speed and governorSteady within the governor class band; no hunting
Bearing temperature and oilStable; oil pressure and temperature within limits; no water in the oil
VibrationAgainst baseline. A sudden change is a shutdown decision, not a monitoring observation.
Exhaust conditionBack pressure or condenser vacuum steady; exhaust temperature within limits
Steam strainer differentialRising differential means a fouling strainer
Trip and throttle valveExercised on the scheduled interval — a valve that has not moved is not a protective device
ShutdownUnload, trip, open drains, keep the auxiliary oil pump running until the rotor has stopped and bearing metal temperature has fallen

14.3Lubrication

14.3.1Oil selection

Table 14.3-1 — Common lubricant grades in rotating equipment
ISO VGViscosity at 40 °CTypical application
VG 3232 cStSteam and gas turbines, high-speed machines, hydraulic systems
VG 4646 cStThe general machinery default — pump and compressor bearing housings, turbine-grade circulating systems
VG 6868 cStHigher load or higher ambient; some gear applications
VG 100 – 220100 – 220 cStGear units; selection per AGMA / vendor requirement
ISO VG is the viscosity at 40 °C, not at operating temperature. Oil thins as it warms. What the bearing needs is a particular viscosity at the bearing, so the grade must be chosen for the actual operating temperature and load. In a 35 °C tropical ambient a bearing housing runs hotter than in a temperate plant, and the grade that worked elsewhere may be too thin here. Follow the vendor's recommendation for the actual conditions rather than copying a grade from another site.

14.3.2Constant level oilers

A constant level oiler holds a fixed oil level in a bearing housing by admitting oil from an inverted reservoir bottle as the level drops.

Table 14.3-2 — Installing and using a constant level oiler
PointDetail
Set the level to the shaftThe oil level must sit at the bottom of the lowest rolling element of the bearing — roughly the centre of the lowest ball or roller. The oiler must be adjusted to achieve that level, not simply bolted on.
Mount on the correct sideFit on the side where shaft rotation throws oil away from the oiler connection. Fitting on the wrong side causes the oiler to read a pressurised level and over-fill or under-fill the housing.
Pressure balanceThe oiler must be vented to the same pressure as the bearing housing. An unbalanced oiler on a housing with a labyrinth seal will not hold level.
Check the bottleA full bottle with no bubbles can mean either a correctly held level or a blocked feed. A bottle that empties quickly means a leak.
Do not over-fillToo much oil churns, overheats and oxidises. Over-filling is as damaging as under-filling and is far more common.
The most frequent constant level oiler fault is a correct-looking bottle over a dry bearing. If the feed passage is blocked or the oiler is mounted at the wrong height, the bottle stays full while the housing runs low. Verify the actual oil level through the sight glass, not the bottle, and confirm the level against the bearing centreline at commissioning.

Table 14.3-4 — Oil mist system considerations
ItemNote
Standby machine protectionThe principal benefit. A spared pump on mist is kept internally dry and clean, which largely eliminates standby corrosion — a major cause of failure on first start.
ReclassifiersSized per bearing. Mist must be condensed into usable droplets at the bearing; the fitting is specific to the bearing type and size.

14.3.4Grease

Table 14.3-5 — Grease practice
PointGuidance
QuantityLess than people think. A rolling element bearing housing is typically filled to about one third to one half of its free volume. Over-greasing churns, overheats and is a leading cause of grease-lubricated bearing failure.
ReliefOpen the grease relief plug while greasing and leave it open until the machine has run and purged excess. Grease pumped into a sealed housing pushes out the bearing shields and can burst seals.
CleanlinessWipe the nipple before connecting. A dirty nipple injects abrasives directly into the bearing.
CompatibilityGreases with different thickeners can be incompatible and will soften or harden when mixed — the mixture can be worse than either alone. Lithium complex and polyurea, in particular, should not be mixed. Purge thoroughly when changing type, and label the machine.
IntervalFrom the bearing manufacturer's relubrication calculation, based on bearing type, bore, speed and temperature. Halve the interval for every 15 K above about 70 K housing temperature as a common rule of thumb.
GradeNLGI 2 for most general industrial bearings; NLGI 3 for vertical shafts where NLGI 2 would slump

14.3.5Oil condition monitoring

Table 14.3-6 — What to monitor and what it indicates
TestIndicates
AppearanceCloudy or milky means water. Dark means oxidation or overheating. Free water is visible in the sight glass bottom.
ViscosityRise means oxidation or contamination with a heavier fluid; fall means dilution with a lighter one — often process fluid leaking in
Water contentWater destroys the oil film and corrodes raceways. Even small, persistent water contamination shortens bearing life markedly.
Acid number (TAN)Oxidation and additive depletion — the oil is approaching the end of its life
Particle count / cleanliness codeISO 4406 code. Circulating systems on API machinery are flushed and maintained to a specified cleanliness level.
Wear metal analysisIron, copper, tin, chromium — identifies which component is wearing before it fails
Water is the contaminant that matters most, and the easiest to overlook. In a humid tropical climate, bearing housings breathe moist air as they heat and cool, and water condenses in the sump. Fitting desiccant breathers or sealed/expansion-chamber breathers on bearing housings is one of the cheapest reliability improvements available, and it directly addresses the dominant local failure mechanism.
