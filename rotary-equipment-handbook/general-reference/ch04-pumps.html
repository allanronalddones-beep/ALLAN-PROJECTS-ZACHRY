4.1Choosing a pump family

The first decision is rotodynamic versus positive displacement. It is driven by viscosity, flow, differential head and whether the flow must be metered.

Table 4.1-1 — Rotodynamic versus positive displacement
Centrifugal (rotodynamic)Positive displacement
Flow vs pressureFlow falls as head rises — follows a curveFlow near-constant regardless of pressure
Viscosity limitDegrades above ~100 cSt, impractical above ~500 cStImproves with viscosity; handles 100 000 cSt+
Low flow, high headPoor — low specific speed, poor efficiencyNatural fit
High flow, low headNatural fitImpractical
Metering accuracyNoYes — API 675 controlled volume
Dead-headSurvivable briefly; heats upDestroys the pump or bursts the line. Relief valve mandatory.
Shear sensitivityHigh shear at impellerGentler — favours emulsions, polymers
PulsationSmoothInherent — needs dampeners and pulsation study
Every positive displacement pump needs a relief valve on the discharge. It will not "find its own operating point". If the discharge is blocked, pressure rises until something fails. The relief must be upstream of any isolation valve and sized for full pump capacity.

4.2API 610 type codes

API 610 classifies pumps by a two-letter configuration code plus a number. The code tells you the bearing arrangement, casing split and mounting without a drawing.

Why VS6 exists. When NPSH available at grade is insufficient, a barrel (can) pump drops the first-stage impeller several metres below grade. Every metre of can depth adds a metre of static head to NPSHa. It is the standard answer for liquids at or near their bubble point — LPG, condensate, light ends.

4.3Affinity laws

For a given pump operating at the same point of its curve, changing speed scales the performance predictably.

Speed change

Q2Q1 = N2N1 H2H1 = (N2N1)2 P2P1 = (N2N1)3

Impeller diameter trim

Q2Q1 ≈ D2D1 H2H1 ≈ (D2D1)2 P2P1 ≈ (D2D1)3
Diameter trim is approximate; speed change is not. The speed laws are exact for geometrically similar flow. Trimming changes the impeller–volute relationship, so the real result falls short of the prediction, increasingly so for large trims. Limit trims to roughly 10–15 % of maximum diameter, and expect an efficiency penalty of around 1 point per 5 % trimmed.
The cube law is why variable speed saves money. Throttling a pump from 100 % to 70 % flow leaves shaft power near 85–90 %. Slowing it to deliver 70 % flow drops power to 0.7³ ≈ 34 %. The saving is real but only where the system curve is friction-dominated; against a high static head, slowing the pump quickly reaches the point where it delivers nothing at all.

50 Hz to 60 Hz

A pump moved from 50 Hz to 60 Hz supply runs 20 % faster:

QuantityRatioChange
Speed1.20+20 %
Flow1.20+20 %
Head1.44+44 %
Shaft power1.73+73 %
NPSH required≈ 1.44+44 %
Relevant to Philippine installations. Equipment built for 50 Hz European practice and installed on a 60 Hz supply absorbs roughly 1.73 times the power and demands about 1.44 times the NPSH. The motor is very likely undersized and the pump may cavitate. Check both before energising.

4.4Specific speed and impeller profile

Specific speed is a dimensionless-in-spirit index that characterises impeller geometry. It is evaluated at the best efficiency point, at maximum impeller diameter, for a single stage and single suction.

Ns= N⁢Q H0.75
N
rotational speed, rpm
Q
flow at BEP — m³/s (metric form) or US gpm (US form)
H
head per stage at BEP — m (metric form) or ft (US form)
Always state which convention. The US form (gpm, ft) gives numbers about 51.6 times larger than the metric form (m³/s, m). A "specific speed of 1500" is meaningless without the units. Vendor literature is usually US.
Table 4.4-1 — Impeller profile by specific speed
Ns (US)Ns (metric)Impeller typeCharacteristicPeak efficiency
500 – 150010 – 29RadialHigh head, low flow. Steep curve.Lower
1500 – 400029 – 78Francis / mixedThe efficiency sweet spotHighest
4000 – 700078 – 136Mixed flowHigh flow, moderate headHigh
7000 – 15000136 – 290Axial (propeller)Very high flow, very low head. Power rises at shutoff.High
Axial pumps behave backwards at shutoff. A radial pump draws least power at closed valve; an axial pump draws most. Never start an axial or high specific speed mixed-flow pump against a closed discharge — the motor will be overloaded. Radial pumps are started closed as standard practice; axial pumps are started open.

4.5Reading the performance curve

Table 4.5-1 — Curve terminology
TermDefinitionWhy it matters
BEPBest efficiency point — flow at which efficiency peaksHydraulic loads are minimum here. Everything is referenced to it.
ShutoffZero flow, closed dischargeMaximum head. All power becomes heat — temperature rise limits run time.
Run-outMaximum flow the pump will passMotor overload risk; NPSHr is highest here.
PORPreferred operating region — API 610: 70 % to 120 % of BEP flowWhere the pump should normally run. Vibration within API limits.
AORAllowable operating region — defined by the vendorWider than POR. Beyond it the vendor accepts no responsibility.
Rated pointThe duty the pump is purchased forAPI 610 requires the rated point to fall within the POR.
Minimum continuous stable flowLowest flow at which the pump runs without unacceptable vibrationSets the minimum flow bypass size
Head rise to shutoffPercentage head increase from rated point to shutoffAPI 610 prefers a continuously rising curve, typically 5–20 % rise, for stable parallel operation and control
Running below minimum flow is the most common way to destroy a pump. At low flow the pump suffers suction and discharge recirculation, high radial load, high vibration, and rapid temperature rise. Oversizing a pump "for safety" and then throttling it into low-flow operation is self-defeating — it guarantees the damage it was meant to avoid.

Temperature rise at low flow

ΔT= g⁢H cp⁢η (1−η)
ΔT
temperature rise across the pump, K
g
9.80665 m/s²
H
head, m
cp
specific heat capacity, J/(kg·K)
η
efficiency at the operating point, as a fraction

At shutoff, η → 0 and all absorbed power heats the contained liquid. This is why a pump must never be left running against a closed valve, and why minimum flow bypasses exist.

4.6Power and efficiency

Hydraulic and shaft power

Pshaft[kW] = Q⁢H⁢SG 367⁢η
Q
flow, m³/h
H
total differential head, m
SG
specific gravity at pumping temperature
η
pump efficiency as a fraction
367
rounded from 367 097 / 1000 — derived from ρwater g / 3600

In US units:

Pshaft[hp] = Q[gpm]⁢H[ft]⁢SG 3960⁢η

Worked example

Duty: 250 m³/h, 85 m differential head, SG 0.82, pump efficiency 76 %.

P = (250 × 85 × 0.82) / (367 × 0.76) = 17 425 / 278.9 = 62.5 kW

API 610 requires the driver to be rated for the maximum power over the AOR with the maximum impeller fitted — not for the rated point. A 75 kW motor is the realistic selection, and the end-of-curve power must be checked before settling on it.

Viscosity correction

Above roughly 20 cSt, head, flow and efficiency all fall relative to the water curve. Correction factors from ANSI/HI 9.6.7 are applied as:

Qvis=CQQwater Hvis=CHHwater ηvis=Cηηwater
Table 4.6-1 — Scale of the viscosity effect (indicative)
ViscosityEffect
< 20 cStNegligible — use the water curve
20 – 100 cStModest correction; efficiency drops a few points
100 – 500 cStSubstantial — efficiency may fall by a third. Power rises.
> 500 cStCentrifugal selection becomes marginal; consider positive displacement
Viscosity cuts head and flow but raises absorbed power. Disc friction on the impeller grows with viscosity. Sizing the motor from the water curve on a viscous duty under-sizes it. Always obtain the vendor's viscous-corrected curve and size the driver from that.

4.7NPSH and suction specific speed

NPSH available is a property of the system; NPSH required is a property of the pump. The system must supply more than the pump demands, with margin.

NPSHa= ps−pv ρ⁢g +Hs −Hf
Table 4.7-1 — NPSH terminology
TermMeaning
NPSH3 / NPSHrThe NPSH at which total head has already dropped by 3 %. This is the value on the vendor curve. The pump is cavitating at NPSH3 — it is a measurement point, not a safe operating limit.
NPSHiIncipient cavitation — the point where cavitation begins. Typically 2 to 20 times NPSH3.
NPSH marginNPSHa − NPSHr, or the ratio NPSHa / NPSHr. What actually protects the pump.
NPSHa = NPSHr is not acceptable. Matching available to required means operating at 3 % head drop with active cavitation. ANSI/HI 9.6.1 gives margin ratios by service; typical practice is a margin of the greater of 1 m or 10 % of NPSHr for benign service, rising substantially for high-energy, high suction-specific-speed or hydrocarbon duties. Project specifications routinely require more.

Suction specific speed

Nss= N⁢Q (NPSH3)0.75

Evaluated at BEP, maximum impeller diameter, per suction eye. A high Nss means a large impeller eye that achieves low NPSHr — but a large eye recirculates badly at part load.

Table 4.7-2 — Suction specific speed guidance (US units: rpm, gpm, ft)
NssInterpretation
< 8 000Conservative. Wide stable operating range.
8 000 – 11 000Normal commercial range. Commonly specified as the upper limit.
11 000 – 14 000Aggressive. Narrowing stable range; justify it.
> 14 000Low NPSHr bought at the cost of part-load reliability. Normally rejected.
The trade-off in one sentence. A vendor can always meet a tight NPSH requirement by enlarging the suction eye — and will quietly hand you a pump that cannot be turned down. Capping Nss on the requisition is how that is prevented.

4.9Sealless pumps

Where a mechanical seal is unacceptable — extreme toxicity, very high leakage cost, or regulatory limits — the seal is eliminated entirely. API 685 governs. Two configurations exist.

Table 4.9-1 — Magnetic drive versus canned motor
Magnetic driveCanned motor
PrincipleTorque transmitted through a containment shell by permanent magnetsMotor rotor runs inside the process liquid, separated from the stator by a thin can
MotorStandard external motorIntegral, special
ContainmentSingle containment shellStator can plus motor housing — secondary containment inherent
Eddy current lossesSignificant in metallic shells; avoided with non-metallic shellsIn the can
FootprintLongerCompact
MaintenanceConventional motor serviceable separatelySpecialist repair
DecouplingMagnets can slip under overload — stalls rather than breaksNot applicable
Both types depend absolutely on liquid for bearing lubrication and cooling. The internal bearings are process-lubricated and the circulation path removes heat from the containment shell. A few seconds of dry running destroys the bearings; a blocked circulation path burns out the shell. Power monitoring and a reliable low-flow trip are not optional extras on a sealless pump — they are part of the pump.
Solids are the other killer. Magnetic particles migrate to the magnets; abrasives destroy the product-lubricated bearings. Sealless pumps need clean liquid, and the circulation path usually needs a filter or cyclone separator.

Acceleration head

Reciprocating pumps accelerate and decelerate the entire suction column every stroke. The inertia of that column imposes an additional NPSH demand that is frequently larger than the friction loss and is routinely overlooked.

ha= L⁢v⁢n⁢C K⁢g
ha
acceleration head, m
L
actual length of the suction line, m (not equivalent length)
v
mean velocity in the suction line, m/s
n
pump speed, rpm
C
constant for the pump configuration — Table 4.10-2
K
fluid compressibility factor — Table 4.10-3
g
9.80665 m/s²
Table 4.10-2 — Configuration constant C
ConfigurationC
Simplex, double acting0.200
Duplex, single acting0.200
Duplex, double acting0.115
Triplex, single or double acting0.066
Quintuplex0.040
Septuplex0.028
Table 4.10-3 — Compressibility factor K
FluidK
Hot oil1.4
Most hydrocarbons1.5
Amine, glycol, water2.0
Deaerated water2.5

Worked example. Triplex pump, 300 rpm, 8 m of suction line, velocity 0.9 m/s, pumping hydrocarbon (K = 1.5).

ha = (8 × 0.9 × 300 × 0.066) / (1.5 × 9.80665) = 142.6 / 14.71 = 9.7 m — an NPSH demand that will dwarf the friction loss and that no centrifugal-pump intuition would predict.

Shorten and fatten the suction line. Acceleration head is proportional to both length and velocity. Doubling the suction pipe diameter cuts velocity by four and acceleration head with it. A suction pulsation dampener close to the pump effectively shortens L to the distance between dampener and cylinder, and is the standard remedy where the line cannot be shortened.

Pulsation

API 674 requires a pulsation analysis for most installations. The approach mirrors API 618 for reciprocating compressors — see §5.5. Design approaches run from an empirical check (Approach 1) through acoustic simulation (Approach 2) to a full mechanical and acoustic study (Approach 3), selected by machine size and criticality.

4.11Cryogenic submerged motor pumps

For LNG, liquid nitrogen, ethylene and similar services, the motor is submerged in the cryogenic liquid inside the pressure containment. There is no shaft penetration and therefore no seal.

In-pot (vessel-mounted)Installed in a dedicated suction pot outside the tankLNG high-pressure send-out, loading
Vaporiser feedIn-pot, multistageRegasification terminals
Table 4.11-2 — Design considerations
FactorRequirement
Motor cooling and lubricationBy the pumped cryogen itself. Flow must be guaranteed before and during running.
Cool-downControlled, slow cool-down before start to avoid thermal shock and differential contraction seizure
MaterialsAustenitic stainless, aluminium alloys, bronze. Carbon steel is excluded — no toughness at −162 °C.
InsulationMotor winding insulation qualified for cryogenic temperature and immersion in the product
Thrust balanceUsually a balance drum or balance disc; thrust bearings run in the cryogen
Minimum flowEssential — loss of flow means loss of motor cooling within seconds
Electrical penetrationCryogenic-rated, gas-tight power feedthrough is a critical containment item
Applicable standard. API 610 does not cover these machines. Cryogenic submerged motor pumps for LNG service are normally specified to the vendor's standard supplemented by project requirements, with reference to EN 1473 and NFPA 59A for the terminal, and IEC 60079 for the electrical aspects of a machine immersed in flammable liquid.
