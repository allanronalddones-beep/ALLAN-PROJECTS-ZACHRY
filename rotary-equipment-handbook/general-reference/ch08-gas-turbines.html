8.3Cycle and performance

The Brayton cycle: air is compressed, fuel is burned at approximately constant pressure, and the hot gas expands through the turbine. The compressor absorbs a large fraction — commonly half to two-thirds — of the turbine's gross output. What is left is useful work.

LHV or HHV — always state which. Gas turbine performance is conventionally quoted on LHV. Fuel is often sold and metered on HHV. For natural gas the difference is roughly 10 %, so an efficiency quoted on HHV looks about 10 % relatively worse than the same machine on LHV. Comparing two vendors on different bases is a real and common error in bid evaluation.

Combined cycle and cogeneration

Exhaust temperature is an asset. A heavy duty frame machine with lower simple-cycle efficiency may beat an aeroderivative overall, because its hotter exhaust makes more and better steam. Select on the efficiency of the whole scheme, not the gas turbine alone.

8.5Site derating

A gas turbine is a volumetric air machine. Anything that reduces the mass of air it ingests reduces its output almost proportionally.

Worked example — tropical site.

A machine with an ISO rating of 30 MW, installed at 50 m elevation with a 32 °C design ambient, 300 Pa inlet loss and 400 Pa exhaust back pressure.

  • Temperature: (32 − 15) × 0.7 % = −11.9 %
  • Altitude: factor 0.994 → −0.6 %
  • Inlet loss: 300 Pa × 0.4 %/100 Pa = −1.2 %
  • Exhaust loss: 400 Pa × 0.25 %/100 Pa = −1.0 %

Combined ≈ −14.4 %, giving roughly 25.7 MW at site conditions — before any allowance for degradation between washes. Sizing the driven equipment from the 30 MW nameplate would have been a 4 MW error.

This matters acutely in the Philippines and the tropics generally. A 32 to 35 °C design ambient costs 12 to 15 % of nameplate output before anything else is counted. Evaporative cooling or inlet chilling recovers part of it; high ambient humidity limits what evaporative cooling can achieve. Always obtain the vendor's site-specific performance curve for the actual design ambient — never scale from the ISO rating by rule of thumb for a final selection.

8.6Fuel and emissions

Table 8.6-1 — Fuel considerations
FuelNotes
Natural gasPreferred. Wobbe index must stay within the combustor's band — a change in gas composition can require re-tuning or prevent operation.
Distillate (diesel)Common backup fuel. Dual-fuel machines transfer on load. Requires fuel treatment and more frequent hot gas path inspection.
Heavy / residual fuelRequires washing and inhibitor dosing for vanadium and sodium. Substantially shortens inspection intervals.
Refinery / process gasVariable composition and heating value. Hydrogen content affects flame speed and combustor design.
Table 8.6-2 — NOx control
MethodPrincipleComment
Dry low NOx (DLN / DLE)Lean premixed combustion lowers flame temperatureStandard for gas firing. Narrower operating band; combustion dynamics must be managed.
Water or steam injectionDiluent lowers flame temperatureEffective on liquid fuel. Costs efficiency and consumes treated water.
SCRAmmonia or urea over a catalyst in the exhaustPost-combustion. Needed where permits demand single-digit ppm.
Always quote emissions with the oxygen reference. "25 ppm NOx" is meaningless alone. The convention is ppmvd corrected to 15 % O₂, dry. Without the reference, two quotations cannot be compared, and a permit limit cannot be demonstrated.
