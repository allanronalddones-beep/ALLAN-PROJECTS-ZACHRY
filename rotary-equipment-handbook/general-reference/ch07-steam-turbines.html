7.2Exhaust and extraction arrangements

Table 7.2-1 — Classification by steam usage
TypeExhaust conditionWhere it fits
Back pressureAbove atmospheric — exhausts into an LP steam headerSteam is needed by the process. The turbine is effectively a pressure-reducing valve that produces work. Highest overall efficiency in a balanced steam network.
CondensingBelow atmospheric, into a surface condenser, typically 0.05 – 0.15 bar aMaximum work per kg of steam. Needs cooling water, a condenser, air ejectors and a vacuum system.
Extraction (controlled)Steam is taken off at an intermediate pressure, held by a control valveSupplies a process header while generating power. Two degrees of freedom — power and extraction flow.
Bleed (uncontrolled extraction)Steam taken off at whatever pressure that stage happens to be atFeedwater heating. Pressure floats with load.
Induction (mixed pressure)Steam admitted at an intermediate stageUses surplus LP steam that would otherwise be vented
Extraction-condensingBothLarge cogeneration sets
The back pressure turbine is nearly free work. If the process needs LP steam and a pressure-reducing valve is letting HP steam down to make it, replacing that valve with a back pressure turbine extracts shaft work from a pressure drop that was being wasted. The steam still arrives at the process, slightly cooler. The economics are usually compelling where a suitable driven machine exists.
Condensing turbines fail on vacuum, not on steam. Loss of condenser vacuum raises exhaust pressure and temperature fast; last-stage blades overheat and the machine trips. Air in-leakage, cooling water loss and ejector failure are the usual causes. Exhaust temperature and condenser pressure are protection points, not just indications.

A Curtis (velocity-compounded) stage is an impulse stage with two rows of moving blades and an intermediate fixed row. It absorbs a large pressure drop in one stage and is commonly used as the first stage of a multistage machine to drop the steam condition quickly and shorten the casing.

Most real machines are mixed. A Curtis or Rateau impulse first stage followed by reaction staging is a common arrangement. The classification matters less for selection than for understanding thrust, which drives the thrust bearing and balance piston design.

Theoretical steam rate

The steam flow an ideal, loss-free turbine would need per unit of work:

Worked example.

Back pressure turbine: inlet 42 bar a / 400 °C, exhaust 4.5 bar a. From steam tables the isentropic drop is about 430 kJ/kg. Overall efficiency 68 %. Required shaft power 1500 kW.

TSR = 3600 / 430 = 8.37 kg/kWh

ASR = 8.37 / 0.68 = 12.3 kg/kWh

Steam flow = 1500 × 12.3 ≈ 18 500 kg/h

Rate the turbine for the worst steam condition, not the normal one. Steam headers sag. A turbine sized at normal inlet pressure and temperature will not make rated power when the header drops during an upset — which is exactly when the driven machine is most needed. API 611 and 612 require the turbine to deliver rated power at the specified minimum inlet and maximum exhaust conditions. Specify that envelope on the datasheet.

7.5The Mollier diagram

How it is used:

  1. Locate the inlet state from inlet pressure and temperature — point 1, in the superheated region.
  2. Drop vertically (constant entropy) to the exhaust pressure line — point 2s.
  3. The vertical distance 1 → 2s is Δhs, the isentropic enthalpy drop.
  4. Multiply by the isentropic efficiency to get the actual drop; the real end state 2 lies to the right at higher entropy.
Exhaust wetness limits the expansion. If point 2 falls below the saturation line, the steam is wet and water droplets erode the last-stage blades. Steam quality at exhaust is normally kept above about 0.88 (12 % moisture). Where the expansion would go wetter, the answer is more inlet superheat, reheat, or a higher exhaust pressure. Note that the real expansion ends drier than the ideal one — the losses reheat the steam.

7.6Governing and overspeed protection

Table 7.6-1 — NEMA SM 23 governor classes
ClassSpeed regulationSpeed variationTypical use
A10 %± 0.75 %Simple mechanical governor, non-critical drives
B6 %± 0.50 %General process drives
C4 %± 0.25 %Compressor drives requiring closer control
D0.50 %± 0.25 %Generator drives, critical speed holding
Regulation and variation are different things. Speed regulation is the steady-state droop from no load to full load. Speed variation is the band the governor holds at a fixed load. A generator drive needs tight variation (frequency); a compressor drive usually needs predictable droop for load sharing.

Overspeed protection

A steam turbine that loses its load accelerates very fast. Protection is layered and each layer must be independent: the governor valve controls speed in normal operation; a separate trip and throttle valve shuts off steam on a trip signal; and an independent overspeed trip device, mechanical or electronic, initiates that trip. API practice sets the overspeed trip at approximately 110 % of maximum continuous speed, with the machine designed to withstand at least 115 %. The trip and throttle valve must be exercised on a defined schedule — a valve that has not moved in two years is not a protective device.
