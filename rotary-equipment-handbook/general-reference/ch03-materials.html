PREN is a screening tool, not an acceptance criterion. It ranks alloys; it does not qualify one for a service. Actual performance depends on temperature, chloride concentration, pH, oxidising species, crevice geometry and — above all — whether the weld and heat treatment were done correctly. Qualify with ASTM G48 testing, not arithmetic.

Duplex has a ceiling that surprises people. Duplex and super duplex are excellent at ambient and moderate temperature but suffer 475 °C embrittlement — a loss of toughness from prolonged exposure well below that figure. Keep continuous service below about 300 °C for 22 Cr and 270 °C for 25 Cr. This rules duplex out of many hot services where its corrosion resistance would otherwise be attractive.
Minimum design metal temperature is not the process minimum. MDMT must account for auto-refrigeration on depressuring, start-up and upset conditions, and ambient. A propane service operating at +20 °C may see −42 °C during a blowdown. Specify the material for the MDMT, not the normal operating temperature.

Sour service hardness is an acceptance criterion, not a property. NACE MR0175 / ISO 15156 caps carbon and low-alloy steel at 22 HRC (approximately 237 HBW), with separate and often lower limits for welds, HAZ and CRAs. Hardness must be verified on the finished component after final heat treatment — not inferred from the material grade.

Galvanic pairing in seawater. Seawater is an efficient electrolyte. Coupling super duplex (noble) to carbon steel or bronze (active) drives rapid attack on the less noble metal. Keep the galvanic series in mind for bolting, wear rings and small internal parts, and isolate dissimilar metals where area ratios are unfavourable — a small anode feeding a large cathode is the worst case.

3.5.2Chloride-bearing service

Chloride stress corrosion cracking (CSCC) is the dominant failure mode for austenitic stainless steels. Three conditions must coexist: a susceptible material, tensile stress, and chlorides with temperature.

Table 3.5-2 — Relative CSCC resistance
MaterialCSCC resistancePractical guidance
304 / 316 austeniticPoorAvoid above ~60 °C where chlorides are present and the part is stressed
904L / 6 Mo austeniticModerateHigher nickel improves resistance substantially
22 Cr duplexGoodUsable well above the austenitic limit
25 Cr super duplexVery goodStandard for severe chloride service
Alloy 625 / 825ExcellentWhere duplex is ruled out by temperature
TitaniumExcellentImmune to CSCC in neutral chlorides
External CSCC under insulation is a real failure mode. Austenitic stainless under wet insulation, with chlorides leached from the insulation itself and the metal running at 50–150 °C, cracks from the outside. It accounts for a substantial share of stainless failures in coastal plant. Coating under insulation, not material upgrade, is normally the fix.

3.5.3Wet H₂S / sour service

Sour service applies where water is present as a liquid phase together with H₂S above threshold partial pressure. Both conditions are required — dry H₂S is not sour service.

pH2S = ptotal,abs × yH2S
pH₂S
partial pressure of H₂S
ptotal,abs
total absolute system pressure
yH₂S
mole fraction of H₂S in the gas phase

ISO 15156-2 sets the threshold for sour service in oil and gas production at an H₂S partial pressure of 0.3 kPa (0.05 psi) absolute with liquid water present. Above that, the standard's material restrictions apply.

B7 and B7M are not the same bolt. A193 Gr B7 is the standard high-strength stud. B7M is quenched, tempered and hardness-controlled to 22 HRC for sour service, with lower allowable stress as a consequence. Substituting B7 for B7M on a sour application is a genuine safety issue, and the two look identical in a bin.

Explosive decompression. In high-pressure gas service, gas dissolves into the elastomer. A rapid depressurisation leaves it with nowhere to go and the O-ring blisters or splits from the inside. Specify an ED-resistant compound (tested to NORSOK M-710 or ISO 23936-2) for gas service above roughly 40 bar with any prospect of rapid blowdown. The base polymer alone does not tell you whether a compound is ED-resistant.
EPDM and oil. The most common elastomer error in the field is fitting EPDM where mineral oil is present. EPDM is excellent in hot water and steam and is destroyed by petroleum oil. A seal flush plan carrying oil, or a lube oil system, must not see EPDM.

3.7Pressure–temperature ratings

A flange "Class" is not a pressure. It is an index into a table whose entries depend on material group and temperature. The same Class 300 flange is rated differently in carbon steel and in 316 stainless.

How a rating is derived

  1. The material is assigned to a material group in ASME B16.5 Table 1A (for example, A105 forged carbon steel is Group 1.1).
  2. That group has a pressure–temperature table (Table 2-1.1 for Group 1.1) listing allowable working pressure at temperature for each Class.
  3. Allowable pressure falls as temperature rises, because the allowable stress of the material falls.
  4. Below the lowest tabulated temperature, the rating is capped at the value for that temperature — it does not continue to rise.

Verify these against the standard before use. Table 3.7-2 is given to show the shape of the derating curve and the size of the effect — Class 150 carbon steel loses two-thirds of its rating between ambient and 400 °C. It is not a substitute for ASME B16.5. Read the actual value for your material group, Class and temperature out of the current edition, and note that B16.5 covers NPS ½ to 24 only; above that, B16.47 Series A or B applies and the ratings differ.
Class 150 is the trap. A Class 150 carbon steel flange is often assumed to be good for "150 psi". At ambient it is good for 285 psi (19.6 bar); at 400 °C it is good for 94 psi (6.5 bar). Hot Class 150 service is where flange rating errors concentrate — and pump discharge nozzles on hot service are exactly that case.
