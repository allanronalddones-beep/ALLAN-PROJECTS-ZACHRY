Once the purchase order is raised, the design effort shifts to the manufacturer and the requisitioning engineer's priorities shift to drawings, delivery schedules, and conformance to the quality standards established during selection and written into the data sheet or purchase order.

It is one thing to make a precise specification; it is another to ensure the manufacturer supplies a product in line with it. Design and fabrication involve many organisational sub-units of the supplier and its sub-vendors, and a lack of communication or commitment at any of them results in non-conformance. That can be rectified later, but rectification is very expensive on a project driven by tight schedules.

Partnership, not policing

An approach based on partnership toward the common goal of the right pump for the application works better than adversarial inspection. It demands regular meetings between purchaser and vendor to ensure proper communication on design engineering, fabrication and quality control — while those activities are still in the manufacturer's domain and can still be influenced cheaply.

Once the product is ready, the question becomes whether the pump really was made to the specification and will stand behind the specified performance when installed. That is the role of an effective testing and inspection plan — which itself needs communication with purchaser, vendor and sub-vendors on the extent of participation and the plan's requirements.

The inspection and testing framework

  • Requirements based on ANSI/ASME B73.1M (horizontal centrifugal pumps), B73.2M (vertical centrifugal pumps), and API 610;
  • specifying the inspection and testing requirements;
  • shop test acceptance criteria;
  • preparation of an inspection and testing checklist;
  • review of the shop performance and procedures;
  • reporting of the test.

These requirements are normally communicated through the pump specification itself.

Material inspection

Four areas: material checks (chemical composition and physical properties), casting defects and their classification, non-destructive testing, and repair procedures for castings and welding.

Material checks

Material specifications for pump components are usually based on API 610. In the 8th Edition, Table H-1 tabulates the material class for various pump components and Table H-2 provides the ASTM specifications. (Table designations change between editions — confirm against the edition in force.)

The purchaser may alternatively specify materials from the liquid properties and reference the applicable ASTM, ANSI, AISI, BS, DIN or equivalent standard. Those standards provide the chemical composition limits and desired physical properties of the grade; ASTM, for example, specifies the tests to be run on the heat from which the material is supplied.

Such tests are specifically recommended where:

  • pump components are exposed to traces of hydrogen sulphide — in which case the materials of specific components must conform to NACE standard MR-01-75 (now MR0175/ISO 15156 — confirm the current designation);
  • pumps are in highly corrosive or hazardous service;
  • pumps are in low temperature applications — below −50 °C, though some specifications take −29 °C as the limit.

Casting defects and classification

Special attention goes to the pressure-containing parts — pump casing and seal housings. These are usually castings, so special requirements apply to ensure component integrity. It is mandatory that castings are free from porosity, cracks, blowholes, shrink holes, scales and any other serious defect.

Where a major defect is found in a casting for cryogenic service or in any special material, carry out the inspection in this order:

  1. check the mill test reports for chemical composition and physical properties;
  2. check any post-repair heat treatment charts;
  3. check or witness the non-destructive examination as required and specified;
  4. check and review the welding procedures.
What ASTM classes as a "major" repair
  • the casting fails the hydrostatic pressure test;
  • the depth of any cavity prepared for welding exceeds 20% of wall thickness or 1 in (25.4 mm), whichever is lesser;
  • the cavity prepared for welding is greater than 10 in² (65 cm²);
  • any repair to a cast iron casting.

Impact testing

Carbon steel materials in low-temperature service below the ductile–brittle transition require careful selection to avoid brittle failure. Where the pumping temperature is −29 °C and below, the selected carbon steel must meet the minimum Charpy V-notch impact energy requirements at the lowest specified temperature in accordance with paragraph UG-84 of ASME Section VIII Div. 1.

Repair procedures

Where a noticed defect qualifies as a major repair, a written procedure must be accepted by the purchaser before the repair is carried out. The procedure must include:

  • how the defect was detected;
  • a sketch or drawing showing the location and depth of the defect;
  • the method of repair, including heat treatment and the inspection procedure after repair.

Peening, plugging or impregnation of a casting is not normally allowed as a repair procedure for ferrous pressure-containing casings. Weldable grades of steel may be repaired in accordance with ASME Section VIII Div. 1 and ASME Section IX. Those codes also apply to other critical welds of pump components. Structural welding not covered by ASME — baseplates and similar — should be welded to AWS D1.1 as a minimum.

Shop tests

Two tests: the hydrostatic test and the shop running test. API classifies acceptance tests as witnessed or observed, and the distinction is contractual, not cosmetic.

Hydrostatic test

  • All pressure-containing parts, including auxiliary components and piping, tested at 1.5 × the maximum allowable pressure anticipated from the pump or system.
  • All cooling water jackets and passages at 7.9 bar g, or as specified by the purchaser.
  • When hydrotesting stainless steel pumps, ensure the chloride content of the test water is less than 50 ppm — otherwise you risk introducing chloride stress corrosion cracking during the very test meant to prove integrity.

Shop running test

Carried out to verify pump performance and the mechanical integrity of the unit — including vibration and noise levels. The hydraulic and mechanical performance requirements must be clearly defined in all applicable specifications and in the data sheets before the bid inquiry is issued.

Not all standards mandate a performance test

ANSI B73.1M and B73.2M do not mandate hydraulic or mechanical performance testing. Because ANSI pumps are manufactured and sold as standard, off-the-shelf products, the standardisation allows the tests to be skipped. Where a test is specified, the Hydraulic Institute Standard or more stringent limits may be imposed.

API 610 makes hydraulic and mechanical performance testing mandatory for all pumps.

Even for ANSI pumps, industry standards recommend a performance test where:

  • pumps are operating in parallel;
  • suction specific speed is more than 11 000;
  • normal flow is less than 10% above the minimum continuous flow rate;
  • an NPSH-r test is required where the NPSH-a to NPSH-r difference is less than 1.8 m.

Performance test procedure

Horizontal and vertical pumps need different test stand configurations. A typical horizontal pump test stand is a closed loop — the same water recirculated for the whole test duration — though open loop configurations exist.

Test stand equipment and instrumentation

  • storage tank or pit for test fluid;
  • slotted base for supporting pumps, drivers and auxiliary equipment of different sizes;
  • suction and discharge piping with globe valves for proper flow control, and a flow meter;
  • a negative and positive pressure source for NPSH testing;
  • pressure indicators on suction and discharge, installed as close as possible to the pump casing;
  • prime mover to drive the pump;
  • instrumentation to accurately measure the power delivered by the prime mover;
  • horsepower monitoring by torque meter or calibrated motor;
  • speed measurement, to check for variation in supply frequency;
  • data collector / analyser / proximity probes to measure and record;
  • decibel meter for noise levels;
  • thermometers for bearing housing temperatures;
  • straightening vanes in the suction, and a cooling coil in the loop.
Calibration — check this first, on arrival

All instrumentation should be calibrated semi-annually or annually depending on the instrument and the shop's experience. A calibration certificate should be available on demand and instruments should carry calibration stickers. Walking the test bay and checking stickers before the test starts is a five-minute task that prevents a worthless test — and uncalibrated instruments are a documented cause of test failure.

Running the test

The pump is mounted on the test base and clamped. Depending on size, rating and test specification, either the vendor's motor or the motor procured for the pump is used; it is aligned to the pump and electrically connected, and the suction and discharge piping is connected. Instrumentation is connected and activated. The pump is started and allowed to stabilise, and readings are taken once pump and motor temperatures flatten out.

Usually five to seven test points are recorded, from near shut-off to 120% of rated flow. The points must include the normal and the rated flow rates. This is achieved by starting the pump with the discharge valve closed — the shut-off condition — then opening the valve in five to seven steps.

Computation and reporting

When collection is complete the data is tabulated. Corrections are computed for speed, density, viscosity and elevation. Corrected flow rates and differential head in metres of liquid column are entered, and power and efficiency are calculated for each point. The computation procedure is in Chapter 03.

Deviations are then checked against the acceptance criteria tolerances, and the tested performance curve is plotted from the collected data.

Causes of shop test failure

When a running test fails the acceptance criteria, corrective action and possibly a retest follow. The common causes divide usefully into real pump problems and measurement problems — and it is worth establishing which before dismantling anything:

Common causes of pump test failure
Real pump problemsMeasurement and interpretation problems
  • incorrect impeller diameter
  • impeller with high residual imbalance
  • unexpected seal failure
  • rubbing at wear rings
  • poor impeller surface quality
  • uncalibrated instruments
  • incorrect data collection and computational errors
  • misinterpretation of test results and acceptance criteria

NPSH testing

NPSH-r testing is done by one of two methods:

  1. Throttling the suction valve to reduce suction pressure;
  2. Pulling a vacuum in the suction vessel.

The pump runs at constant flow rate and constant speed while suction pressure is reduced by either method. The reduction in suction head leads to a reduction in discharge head. For one flow rate, 5 to 12 readings may be taken and plotted; the set is then repeated for 1 to 4 flow rates.

NPSH-r is read off as the suction head at which discharge head drops 3% from the rated head at that flow.

Multistage pumps

For a multistage pump the 3% drop is not taken on total head. The head considered is that of the first stage, obtained approximately by dividing total pump head by the number of stages. Taking 3% of total head would give a far more lenient — and wrong — result.

If the pump is dismantled to correct its NPSH-r, that calls for a retest.

Dismantling after test

Some standards specify that the pump be dismantled after the shop running test, generally to inspect the bearings and mechanical seal. However, if the pump has performed satisfactorily during the shop test, dismantling can be skipped.

Dismantling after test is required only in special circumstances:

  • inconsistent test results;
  • abnormal noise;
  • higher-than-expected vibration;
  • a prototype design.
What the shop test actually buys you

Reviewing hydraulic and mechanical performance by shop test ensures there are no unexpected deviations or ambiguity about performance after installation and commissioning. More practically: once the performance curve has been established at the shop test, that curve tends to remain consistent for the life of the machine so long as there is no mechanical damage or wear.

So when a commissioning anomaly appears and the pump has not been dismantled or mechanically altered, the shop test curve is your evidence that the discrepancy lies in the system layout or system components — not the pump. That is exactly the argument you need in a commissioning dispute, and you only have it if the test was done and documented properly. See Chapter 08.

Correct and accurate pump specification, inspection and testing are the building blocks of reliable operation. The next block is installation.
