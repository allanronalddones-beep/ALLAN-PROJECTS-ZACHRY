For some duties it is enough to buy a pump off the shelf on the horsepower rating of its motor or its overall dimensions. The fit to the application will not be ideal, but the penalty for a mismatch is small.

In process industries that calculus reverses. Pumps perform vital functions continuously, in difficult environments, and the penalties attached to downtime and maintenance are significant. It becomes necessary to specify the process and its requirements properly, so that the selected pump is designed and manufactured to operate under the specified conditions reliably, efficiently and cost-effectively.

Why the specification is the highest-leverage document

A good pump specification is the foundation of pump reliability across its entire service life. It is the first document a user prepares for a pump, and it is often the most important. Failure to define or specify the pump completely for the anticipated process conditions consistently results in poor operating experience and high maintenance costs.

Omissions and ambiguity at this stage prove very expensive later — and the cost is paid in operating budget for twenty years, not in the capital estimate where it would be noticed.

Preparing a specification is a multidisciplinary effort involving process engineers, mechanical engineers, contractors and pump vendors. It is preceded by a system analysis covering the hydraulic aspects, and a statement of mechanical requirements. Once laid out, it is followed by a bid request, a bid analysis, and finally selection.

In any pump specification these process requirements are of prime importance:

  • maximum differential head at the specified flow rates;
  • available Net Positive Suction Head, NPSH-a;
  • anticipated flow range, or the required flow flexibility;
  • liquid properties — hazard potential, specific gravity, vapour pressure and viscosity across the range of pumping temperatures, composition, and the possibility and properties of solids;
  • anticipated transient conditions.

These drive the detailed system analysis, and often dictate mechanical requirements directly. A liquid generating carcinogenic vapours, for instance, may demand a sealless pump rather than one with mechanical seals.

The opposite failure mode

It is equally important not to be over-conservative. Excessive conservatism selects pumps that both mismatch the process and cost more through oversizing. Padding every allowance does not produce a safe design; it produces a pump that spends its life throttled, far right of BEP, carrying high radial load.

System analysis

The first step. It comprises four evaluations: pump boundary conditions, flow requirements, fluid specification, and criticality of service.

Pump boundary conditions

NPSH available and margin

The first boundary condition concerns the suction. From Chapter 03, NPSH-a depends on:

  • vapour pressure of the liquid;
  • pipe losses;
  • pressure in the suction vessel;
  • gradient height;
  • absolute (atmospheric) pressure.

Location matters more than people expect

Absolute pressure is constant for a location — which means you must know where the pump will be installed. This is a live issue when process and pump specifications are prepared for plants erected elsewhere in the world. Elevation above mean sea level determines the absolute pressure available; ambient temperature range and annual rainfall also determine some pump features.

Suction line heat gain

On pumps with a long suction pipe, heat added by heat-tracing lines and by ambient conditions raises the pumping temperature — and with it the vapour pressure of the liquid. Higher vapour pressure reduces NPSH-a. It may therefore be prudent to specify insulation on the suction piping, which is a specification decision, not a construction one.

Fouling

Long suction pipelines bring pipe losses, which are easy to compute. But some applications must also anticipate fouling of pipes, fittings and strainers. The extent of fouling before a cleanout must be estimated and accounted for — it both raises system resistance and drops NPSH-a.

Fouling and Qmax

Fouling in the suction header steepens the droop in the NPSH-a curve, which reduces Qmax — the maximum flow at which a positive NPSH margin still exists. Always evaluate NPSH-a at Qmax, because frictional pipe losses are at their maximum there, and at the minimum suction vessel level, because that gives the minimum suction column height.

The minimum level to use is the level at which an alarm is placed, or the height at which the suction pipe draws off from the vessel — whichever applies. Structural design and process operational limits both bear on this; the limiting guideline is the minimum level at which the maximum flow rate is expected.

Once NPSH-a is known across the operating range, compute the margin against the margin ratio guidelines. Remember that the HI definition of NPSH-r does not adequately cover high suction energy pumps — the damage-free NPSH-r could be 2 to 20 times the 3% head-drop value.

Suction specific speed

Where only minimal NPSH-a is available, note it clearly so that vendors offer pumps with low NPSH-r. But when they do, evaluate the suction specific speed of what is offered. A vendor can always buy you NPSH-r by enlarging the impeller inlet — and sell you a pump that cannot be turned down.

As covered in Chapter 03, Nss above 11 000 brings hydraulic and mechanical problems, especially at flows away from BEP. The basis is a statistical study of 480 refinery pumps over five years, presented by Jerry L. Hallam in 1982.

The honest state of the Nss debate

At the 13th International Pump Users Symposium (Texas A&M, 1996), Bernd Stoffel and Ralf Jaeger presented Experimental Investigations in Respect to the Relevance of Suction Specific Speed for the Performance and Reliability of Centrifugal Pumps. Three standard pumps of different specific speeds and sizes, from three German manufacturers, were each fitted alternately with a high-Nss impeller and one below the 11 000 limit.

The measured quantities serving as indicators for dynamic loading of bearings and seals did not show trends or cause effects in the high-Nss pumps that would indicate higher failure probability. So a genuine debate exists over rejecting pumps above 11 000.

Until there is a clear consensus, it is advisable to be conservative and stay within the recommended limit — but know that the limit is a strong empirical heuristic, not a settled physical law, and be prepared to discuss it with a vendor on those terms.

System resistance and differential head

NPSH-a evaluation more or less settles the suction side. The discharge side must be worked out the same way, considering:

  • static head at the discharge — downstream pressure in the discharge vessel;
  • the height that must be overcome to reach the discharge vessel;
  • the rate of increase of system resistance with flow.

That third item is easy to overlook and consequential. A quickly rising system resistance curve may preclude a maximum flow rate that is required occasionally — particularly where the maximum flow is far above the normal flow.

Control valve sizing interacts with the pump

Control valve sizing is based on the rate of rise of the system resistance curve as well as the pump size. The valve must provide the artificial head loss at the rated and minimum flow rates, and the minimum loss at the maximum flow rate.

One way to flatten the system resistance curve is simply to install a larger pipe size — a decision that has to be made at specification stage, because nobody re-pipes a system to recover pump margin later.

Evaluating suction and discharge resistance together gives the differential head required from the pump.

Flow requirements

Flow is determined by process demand. A specification normally states two flow rates:

Normal versus rated flow
DefinitionTypical relationship
Normal flow rate The flow at which the pump will usually operate. Baseline
Rated flow rate The flow guaranteed by the vendor for the specified operating conditions. 10% above normal for low to medium flows; 5% above normal for higher flows

Rated flow should reflect the maximum the system can envisage under current consideration, and should account for any anticipated future increase in process throughput.

The minimum flow requirement may conflict with the rated flow. Where it does, make provision for recycle of the process liquid. Where possible, state the periods for which the pump will operate at minimum, maximum and rated flows — a long period at low flow implies higher radial loads, which greatly affects bearing life.

Beyond minimum and maximum, the pump may be physically or hydraulically shut off at the discharge under certain operating conditions. Where that is the case, consider the minimum flow provisions from Chapter 05.

Parallel operation deserves special care

If the pump is required to operate in parallel with another, take great care to ensure minimum stable flows — especially where the Q–H curves are dissimilar. Below a certain flow, the pump with the lower shut-off head will begin operating at shut-off. The same happens when curves are very flat and one pump's shut-off head is slightly lower than the other's.

This is why API 610 specifies that pumps of one or two stages operating in parallel must have rising Q–H curves with a 10–20% head rise from rated capacity to shut-off. For three or more stages a slightly lesser rise is allowed, since the same percentage would give excessively high shut-off heads.

Flow requirement is probably the single factor demanding the most team effort to settle definitively. It determines pump sizing and reliability, and pumps specified with flow rates that match actual operation closely have lower life cycle costs.

Fluid specification

Provide as much information about the liquid as possible. Liquid properties determine the pump's:

  • material of construction;
  • design features such as support type and cooling water jackets;
  • impeller design;
  • mechanical seal, sealant and piping plan;
  • construction features such as wear plates and hard coatings;
  • driver horsepower.

Dissolved gases Potassium carbonate solution, for example, can evolve CO₂ at certain pumping temperatures. Gas evolution causes cavitation and affects the pump's ability to build pressure. A suction vent joining an upstream vessel can help.

Criticality of service

Criticality determines both the governing standard and the maintenance philosophy later. A pump is critical where any of the following apply:

  • failure can affect plant safety and there is no standby;
  • the pump is vital for plant operation and its shutdown will curtail the process;
  • it is part of a large horsepower train, where better operation can save energy or improve yields;
  • capital cost is high, and it is very expensive to repair or has a long repair lead time;
  • it is a perennial "bad actor" — a machine that wrecks on the slightest provocation of an off-duty operation.

In most such cases a standby pump is specified, for a critical process on continuous operation.

A specification trick worth knowing

Sometimes the pump and its standby are specified with different types of driver — one on a steam turbine, the other on an electric motor. Process steam can then be utilised and power conserved, and the scheme ensures continued operation even on loss of power supply. Two benefits from one decision.

The data sheet

The data sheet is the pump specification document: an organised format presenting the information from the studies above to the contractor or vendor, with notes covering various aspects and the compulsory or optional features desired. A blank format for centrifugal pumps is available in API 610, and users commonly modify it to suit their own practice.

What to demand on the blank pages

The additional-information pages are where a specification earns its keep. Use them to require the vendor to:

  • declare any deviations from the specified standard;
  • state start-up and minimum spares requirements;
  • quote the pump minimum flow and its basis — not just a number;
  • specify the impeller to volute cutwater clearance for pumps developing more than 200 mlc (this is Gap-B, and it determines whether the pump will have vane-pass vibration for its whole life);
  • state requirements for any special seals and bearings, and their manufacturers;
  • specify noise limits;
  • state requirements for rotordynamic studies — lateral analysis, or torsional vibration analysis of the full train;
  • provide mill reports for specified materials;
  • submit any special welding and attachment procedures;
  • state wear plate or hard coat requirements;
  • state painting and packaging requirements;
  • confirm shipment details;
  • supply pump information in both soft and hard copy.

Bid request

The bid request sends the data sheet to vendors for a quote. Guidelines:

  • Send to three to five qualified vendors. Where the pump falls within standard offerings, three bids are sufficient. For specialised pumps, three to six.
  • Enclose a covering form listing the mandatory and optional documents and their order. This acts as a checklist for the vendor and directly assists the bid evaluation that follows.
  • Consider the user's existing inventory of pump spares when selecting vendors — similar pumps may be quoted that demand no additional spares.
  • Conversely, a vendor may be excluded purely on poor follow-up and spares delivery. That is a legitimate commercial criterion.
Professional courtesy that is also self-interest

Preparing a bid consumes time, effort and money for the vendor, and reviewing it costs the user. Forward the bid request only to vendors whose bids will be seriously considered if their pumps meet the requirements. Padding the bid list to look diligent wastes everyone's budget and degrades the quality of the responses you get.

Bid review and analysis

A clear, comprehensive specification is what lets a purchaser compare bids on an equal basis. Exceptions taken by vendors must be weighed and factored against the desired features and the prices offered. An analytical approach demands a tabulation of bids.

Hydraulic performance

  • percentage of rated flow to the flow at BEP;
  • pump numbers — specific speed and suction specific speed;
  • NPSH-r, and NPSH-a margin at rated and maximum flows;
  • percentage head rise from rated flow to shut-off;
  • pump efficiency at rated and normal flow rates;
  • minimum continuous / stable flow;
  • maximum hydraulic power;
  • noise levels.

  • pump type;

  • cooling water jackets for seal housing, bearing housing or pedestals;
  • impeller size, and the minimum and maximum sizes possible in the volute casing — this is your future headroom for re-rating;
  • single or double volute;
  • material compliance;
  • mechanical seal type and materials offered;
  • bearing types and lubrication;
  • coupling type;

Weighting and the decision

Bid analysis almost never produces a clear winner. Some pumps have an advantage in certain features and a disadvantage in others. The approach is to give weightage to all the factors and arrive at the pump scoring the most marks. Factorial weighting itself depends on many things — and a correct system analysis provides the sound basis for the weighting factors. Standard computer bid-analysis spreadsheets make the comparison convenient and accurate.

Practical sequence
  1. System analysis — boundary conditions, flow, fluid, criticality.
  2. Decide the governing standard from the criticality assessment.
  3. Complete the data sheet, including the blank-page demands.
  4. Issue to three to five vendors with a document checklist.
  5. Tabulate hydraulic, construction, driver and price comparisons.
  6. Apply weightings justified by the system analysis, and award.
  7. Carry the specification forward into the inspection and test plan — the specification is what you will hold the vendor to.

A clear understanding of the pump's operating and system requirements leads to selection of a pump that will be efficient and reliable. To lower pump life cycle costs it is essential to specify and select correctly.
