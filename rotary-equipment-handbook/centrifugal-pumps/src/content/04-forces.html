A centrifugal pump delivers a wide range of flows and heads. The operating point on the curve, and the internal pressures that result, produce axial and radial forces on the rotor. Those forces select the bearings, size the shaft, and — through shaft deflection — determine how long the seals last. This chapter covers where they come from and how each construction manages them.

Axial thrust

Axial thrust arises from internal pressures acting on the exposed areas of the rotating element. It looks like it should be simply the net of discharge and suction pressure times the exposed impeller area — and that is indeed the basis — but several variables are not captured by that approach:

  • location of the impeller relative to the casing wall;
  • impeller shroud symmetry;
  • surface roughness of the walls;
  • wear ring clearance;
  • geometry of the balancing holes.
Calculated thrust is approximate

Many of these variables are indeterminate, so calculated axial forces are at best approximate. In critical applications, during prototype testing, or when troubleshooting repeated thrust bearing failures, install thrust-measuring devices and record internal pressures directly. If the measured results are unacceptable, the design needs modification — not a bigger bearing.

Assumptions behind the approximate method

  • The impeller profile is symmetrical.
  • The impeller centreline coincides reasonably with the volute centreline — within 0.8 mm.
  • Pressure on the front cover and back shroud is equal and constant from the impeller OD down to the wear ring diameter.
  • The parabolic pressure reduction across the shrouds is ignored; the average pressure acting on the shrouds is taken as ¾ of discharge pressure.

The momentum term

Every case includes FX, the axial force from the change in fluid momentum between inlet and outlet:

Single-stage overhung impeller pumps

These "simple" pumps are the most complex to analyse because of the number of impeller and mating-component variants. The convention below follows the source: NA denotes thrust on the non-pressure (suction) side and A the thrust on the pressure side; the net axial thrust is the difference between the two.

Closed impeller, front wear ring only

With perfect impeller symmetry, axial forces above diameter D₁ are balanced — pressure is assumed equal on both sides. The net thrust comes from the pressure difference acting on the annulus between the front wear ring and the shaft.

NA = AS × PS + FX
A = ¾ × PD × (A₁ − AS)

front wear ring diameter
DE

DS

PS, PD

Closed impeller, wear rings both sides, with balancing holes

Balancing holes drilled through the back shroud reduce the pressure in the region under the back wear ring to approximately suction pressure. In practice studies indicate the pressure there is 2–10% of the differential pressure, depending on hole diameter. The equations below assume total balancing hole area is eight times the area between the wear rings.

When D₁ = D₂:
Axial thrust = AS × PS − [0.03 × PD × (A₁ − AS)] + FX

When D₁ ≠ D₂:
NA = AS × PS − [0.03 × PD × (A₁ − AS)] + FX
A = ¾ × PD × (A₁ − A₂)

Closed impeller with back vanes, no back wear ring

Where a back wear ring is not wanted, back vanes are cast integrally on the back shroud. Rotating with the impeller, they act as a small impeller themselves, expelling liquid from inner to outer diameter and so reducing the pressure behind the impeller.

The weakness of back vanes

Back vane performance depends entirely on the clearance between the vane tip and the casing (dimension a − h), normally held at 0.4 to 1.0 mm. As that gap opens up in service, pressure on the back shroud rises and axial thrust increases — so the method is unreliable over time. It is also less efficient than back wear rings: back vanes can cost as much as 3% of pump efficiency.

Open / semi-open impeller with front wear ring

NA = PD × [AS + 2(PS − A₃) ⁄ 3] + FX (form per source)
A = ¾ × PD × (A₃ − AS)

A₃
area of the impeller

With back vanes added, the term [0.00253 × (AR − AS) × ρ × (UR² − US²)] enters as in the closed-impeller-with-back-vanes case.

Because the front cover is absent, the clearance between impeller vanes and casing is critical; where back vanes are also present, the back clearance matters too. Two critical clearances in one assembly compounds the assembly problem considerably.

Double suction impellers

Theoretically a double-suction impeller develops almost no axial thrust, by symmetry. In practice construction is never perfectly symmetrical, so some thrust always exists and a simple thrust bearing absorbs it.

Alignment is not optional here

The symmetry that gives the double-suction impeller its balance requires the impeller outlet to stay aligned with the volute centre. Violate that and substantial thrust is generated — proportional to the axial displacement. This is a direct assembly and overhaul check item.

Multistage pumps — stacked (in-line) impellers

With all impellers stacked facing the same direction, the axial thrust pushing the rotor toward the suction can be very high. Such designs carry a balancing drum or piston: a rotating disk running against a stationary disk or ring at close clearance. The liquid pressure drops across that labyrinth passage, and the leakage is piped back to the pump suction.

Pressure difference across the drum equals the net of discharge and suction pressure, and the resulting force opposes the thrust on the rotor. The arrangement also reduces the pressure acting on the stuffing box. The cost is efficiency: liquid recirculating through the balance line is lost work.

Multistage pumps — back-to-back impellers

Arranging impellers back to back simplifies axial balance for odd or even stage counts. Two locations on the rotor each break down half the total pump head: the centre bushing between the back-to-back impellers, and the throat bush in the high-pressure stuffing box. Sleeve diameters at those two locations can be sized to balance any number of stages.

Practical rules for this arrangement:

  • It is preferable to leave some residual axial thrust so the bearing stays lightly loaded and the rotor is held in one place rather than allowed to float.
  • An increase in wear ring or bush clearance does not affect axial thrust in this design — a genuine reliability advantage over the stacked arrangement.
  • All impeller rings should be the same diameter.
  • On even-stage pumps, the centre sleeve diameter should equal the sleeve diameters in each stuffing box.
  • To balance an odd number of stages, the simplest solution is to change only the centre sleeve diameter.

Radial loads

In an end-suction pump with an overhung impeller, hydraulic radial load comes from non-uniform liquid velocity within the casing, which produces a non-uniform pressure distribution around the impeller circumference.

The volute design is the dominant influence. The volute is sized for flow at BEP, and at that flow velocity and pressure distribution in the casing are uniform, so radial thrust is negligible. The small residual load at BEP comes from the cutwater, which necessarily disturbs the pressure distribution; that force is directed toward the cutwater.

At flows above or below BEP the magnitude of the radial force increases — and its direction also changes.

Radial load behaviour by casing type
CasingUniform atRadial load character
Single volute BEP Near zero at BEP, maximum at shut-off. Unbalanced force can be taken as acting 240° from the cutwater, toward the impeller centre.
Double volute Broadly across the range Substantially reduced everywhere — the rib creates a second cutwater and near-symmetry around the impeller. Some residual remains because the two discharge passages are not symmetrical.
Concentric casing Zero flow Uniformity is progressively lost as flow rises; non-uniformity at BEP is much higher than at no-flow. The opposite of a volute.
Vaned diffuser Broadly across the range Velocity distribution around the impeller is more uniform, so radial loads are much lower.

At shut-off, for an end-suction single-volute pump:

RSO = KSO × (HSO × ρ ⁄ 2.31) × D₂ × B₂

D₂

B₂

At other operating points:

R = RSO × (K ⁄ KSO) × (H ⁄ HSO)
where K = KSO × [1 − (Q ⁄ QBEP)x]

x
exponent from 0.7 to 3.3, established by test. A linear relationship may be safely assumed between 0.7 at Ns 500 and 3.3 at Ns 3500.

A simpler approximate variant:

R = K × (H × ρ ⁄ 2.31) × D₂ × B₂   where K = 0.36 × [1 − (Q ⁄ QBEP)²]

Units as above. Note the squared flow ratio and the fixed 0.36 coefficient — this version hides the specific-speed dependency, so prefer the full form where Ns is known.

Non-hydraulic sources of radial load

  • Vane pass excitation. A fluctuating radial load arises when the clearance between impeller vane tips and the volute cutwater is too small. The resulting vibration occurs at the vane pass frequency = number of impeller vanes × pump running speed. This is a diagnostic signature, and the cure is a correct Gap-B.
  • Mechanical imbalance of the rotor generates a radial force at running speed.
  • Uneven flow through the impeller can itself cause impeller imbalance.
  • Cavitation, and misaligned belts or couplings, also contribute radial load to the shaft.

Shaft deflection

A radial load — hydraulic or from mechanical imbalance — bends the shaft downward in one position. Rotate the shaft 180° and it still bends downward in the same way. That bending under a constant unidirectional load is deflection: the deflected shaft rotates about its own centreline even though that centreline is not straight.

The consequence is a reversal of stress in the shaft on every revolution, which leads to fatigue cracking and eventual shaft breakage.

Deflection versus whip

Shaft whip is a different motion: the shaft end orbits, generating a cone, and the shaft centreline itself shifts 180° for every 180° of shaft rotation. Whip comes from rotordynamic problems; radial load contributes little to it. The two are regularly confused, and they have different cures.

Why deflection must be limited

Rubbing contact. Rotating parts may touch stationary parts. Stainless steel parts gall as soon as rubbing contact is made, which shows up as motor overload. Galling tends to get progressively worse and can end in complete seizure of the rotating element. The first surfaces to touch are the wear rings — whose clearance is deliberately minimised to maximise the pressure drop across them — or, on pumps without wear rings, the shaft against the stuffing box throat bush.

Packing leakage. Conventional packing adjusted for the deflection at one operating condition will not readjust when the deflection changes. Set the packing at the high-capacity end of the curve, then throttle the discharge: the shaft moves from one extreme position to the other, leaving a gap between packing and shaft. That gap plus the elevated stuffing box pressure near shut-off produces excessive leakage.

Seal face misalignment. On a mechanically sealed pump, deflection causes facial misalignment of the seal faces. The faces either open up or mate unevenly, producing uneven and early wear and then leakage.

Calculating deflection

Deflection is calculated by treating the shaft as a cantilever beam, using an expanded beam formula. For a shaft with three major diameters in the overhung section:

δ = (P ⁄ 3E) × [ A³⁄IA + (B³−A³)⁄IB + (C³−B³)⁄IC + (L³−C³)⁄IL ]

δ
shaft deflection
P
radial resultant force
E
Young's modulus — modulus of elasticity of the shaft material
A, B, C, L
distances from the impeller to each diameter change
IA…L
moments of inertia at the respective diameters

Consistent units throughout. The form above is the general expanded cantilever expression; confirm the exact grouping against your own reference before using it for a design calculation.

The L³/D⁴ ratio — and why not to trust it

The formula reduces to the statement that deflection δ is proportional to L³/D⁴, where L is shaft length and D is shaft diameter. Pumps with a lower L³/D⁴ exhibit lower deflection than those with a higher ratio.

Do not use L³/D⁴ to compare vendors

It is widely quoted in bid comparisons and it is a poor basis for one. L³/D⁴ should not be used as a yardstick to compare the reliability of one pump against another. Ask instead for the actual shaft deflection values computed by the OEM at the most severe flow conditions, and compare those.

From forces to bearing selection

Hydraulic loads depend on impeller type and size, casing type, operating point, and suction and discharge pressures. Magnitude and direction both vary greatly with any of those. A complete shaft analysis considers axial and radial forces together and resolves them into reaction forces at the bearing locations.

RB = Fr × (A + B) ⁄ B  ·  RA = Fr − RB

Fr
radial force at the impeller
A, B
distances from the impeller to bearing A, and between bearings

At location A there is only a radial bearing, so it carries PA = RA.

At location B double-row angular contact bearings carry radial and axial load together, so the equivalent load uses the bearing manufacturer's X and Y factors:

PB = X·RB + Y·Fax

X, Y
radial and axial load factors from the bearing catalogue for that bearing type and arrangement
Fax
net axial thrust

Only once the forces on each bearing are known can the bearings be selected properly — and bearing selection is what converts the hydraulic design into achievable availability.

What this chapter means on the plant

Nearly every force discussed here is minimised at BEP and grows as the duty moves away from it. Radial load, shaft deflection, seal face misalignment, bearing load and recirculation all worsen together in the same direction. That is why the allowable operating region is not a bureaucratic restriction: it is the envelope within which the rotor forces stay inside what the shaft, seal and bearings were designed to carry.
