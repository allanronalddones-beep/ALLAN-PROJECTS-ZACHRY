What a pump does, and the two ways of doing it

Every pump spends energy to raise, move or pressurise a liquid. There are only two fundamentally different ways to achieve it, and the distinction governs almost everything else about the machine's behaviour.

A positive displacement pump traps a fixed volume of liquid and pushes it out mechanically. Flow is set by geometry and speed, essentially independent of the pressure it is working against, so the machine will keep raising pressure until something gives. Positive displacement pumps are self-priming, handle high viscosity well, and need relief protection on the discharge.

A roto-dynamic pump — the centrifugal family — adds velocity energy to the liquid with a rotating impeller, then converts that velocity into pressure in a stationary collector. Because the energy transfer depends on the velocity triangles at the impeller, flow and head are coupled: increase the head the pump works against and flow falls away on its own. That self-regulating characteristic is why a centrifugal pump can be safely throttled and why it dominates process plant.

Why this matters in practice

A centrifugal pump cannot generate more than its shut-off head, so a closed discharge valve produces no flow rather than a burst pipe — although the liquid churning in the casing will heat up fast. A positive displacement pump in the same situation will keep building pressure. Protection philosophy differs accordingly.

Positive displacement families

Reciprocating. A plunger or piston reciprocating in a cylinder, with suction and discharge valves in the head. Gland packing contains the pressurised fluid. Plungers are commonly driven in pairs or threes off a common crankshaft (duplex, triplex) to smooth the delivery.

Diaphragm. Fundamentally a plunger pump in which the plunger pressurises hydraulic oil, and the oil flexes a diaphragm that does the actual pumping. The process liquid never touches the drive, which is why diaphragm pumps are the default for hazardous and toxic duties. Critical services use a double diaphragm with a thin water film between the two and a pressure sensor in that gap: normally the gap pressure is zero, and when either diaphragm ruptures the sensor immediately sees full process discharge pressure. The process liquid still has not reached atmosphere.

Rotary. Gear (external and internal), lobe, vane, progressive cavity, peripheral and screw. Each traps liquid in a cavity formed between rotating elements and the casing and carries it from suction to discharge.

Rotary positive displacement types and where each suits
TypeWorking principleSuits
External gear Two identical meshing gears, each on its own bearing-supported shaft. Liquid is carried in the tooth pockets and expelled as the teeth re-mesh. High-pressure hydraulics. Support on both sides of each gear keeps noise low.
Internal gear A gear-within-a-gear: an outer rotor and an inner idler, with a crescent dividing suction from discharge. Only two moving parts. Very low to very high viscosity, smooth non-pulsating flow. Self-priming and can run dry. Reversible.
Lobe Like an external gear pump, but each lobe is driven by external timing gears so the lobes never touch. Bearings sit outside the pumped liquid. Food and solids-laden duties — handles the largest particle size of any PD type without damaging the product.
Vane A slotted rotor eccentric in a cycloidal cam; vanes are thrown out against the cam wall by centrifugal force, hydraulic pressure or pushrods. Low-viscosity, non-lubricating liquids. Excellent suction characteristics from the tight vane/rotor/cam seal.
Progressive cavity A single metal rotor turning inside an elastomer-lined stator; chambers form and travel axially toward the discharge. Wastewater, waste processing, pulp and paper, mining, cosmetics — fluids carrying non-abrasive inclusions.
Peripheral (regenerative) Many small radial vanes on both impeller faces running in a concentric casing; a vortex between vanes and casing transfers the energy. Low flow at high pressure with clean liquids. Tolerates entrained gas well, but inefficient and a poor self-primer.
Screw (two or three spindle) Intermeshing screws in a close-fitting casing move liquid axially. Marine and offshore fuel, burner, lube and hydraulic service. Self-priming, efficient, quiet, pulsation-free, good with high viscosity.

Roto-dynamic (centrifugal) families

Centrifugal pumps are divided by the direction the liquid leaves the impeller, which in turn is set by specific speed:

  • Radial flow — the plain centrifugal pump, usually with a volute casing. Liquid enters at the impeller eye and is thrown radially outward. Low flow, high head.
  • Mixed flow — the discharge direction lies between radial and axial.
  • Axial flow — the rotor is a propeller and flow runs parallel to the shaft. High flow, low head.

Other kinetic machines exist — electromagnetic, jet, gas-lift and hydraulic ram pumps — but they are outside the scope of this reference.

Why centrifugal pumps dominate

In a typical petroleum plant, 80–90% of all installed pumps are centrifugal. The reasons are structural rather than historical: simple construction, good efficiency over a wide flow and head range, smooth flow, and straightforward operation and maintenance. The basic machine is a volute and an impeller on a shaft carried by bearings in a bearing housing, with a coupling at the free end and a seal where the shaft passes through the casing.

The modern centrifugal impeller traces to Denis Papin in 1689, who understood that vanes inside a spiral casing could create a forced vortex, though his vanes were straight. Combs published on curved vanes in 1838; Andrews introduced a proper volute casing in 1839 and a fully shrouded impeller in 1846. John Appold's systematic experiments culminated in his 1851 curved-blade pump, which reached 68% efficiency — a threefold improvement that established blade curvature as the key variable. The centrifugal pump only displaced positive displacement machines broadly once electric motors, steam turbines and internal combustion engines gave it a suitable high-speed driver.

Normal operating speeds are 1500/1800 rpm and 3000/3600 rpm, corresponding to four- and two-pole motors on 50 and 60 Hz supplies. Specialised high-speed designs run from 5000 up to 25 000 rpm.

Classifying centrifugal pumps by construction

Pump data sheets, standards and vendor catalogues all describe a machine along the same handful of construction axes. Knowing them lets you read a bid or an API 610 designation without a catalogue.

Shaft connection Separately coupled or close-coupled Close-coupled pumps mount the impeller directly on an extended motor shaft — no coupling, no alignment, but motor removal to reach the impeller.
Shaft sealing Packed, mechanically sealed or sealless Governs fugitive emission performance and the whole auxiliary piping plan.

In-line (monobloc) pumps deserve separate mention: suction and discharge flanges lie on one axis so the pump mounts directly between pipe runs, needing no baseplate of its own.

Sealless pumps

Every seal is a potential leak path, which is unacceptable for some liquids at any leak rate. Sealless designs eliminate the dynamic seal entirely by containing the whole rotor inside a pressure boundary.

Canned motor pumps put the impeller, shaft and motor rotor inside the pump casing and a containment shell. The stator sits outside the shell and its rotating flux passes through the shell to drive the rotor. The hazardous liquid is confined by the shell and casing and also serves as the rotor coolant and bearing lubricant.

Magnetic drive pumps keep the impeller, shaft and a set of driven magnets inside the containment shell. Drive magnets on a separate shaft outside the shell, coupled to the prime mover, turn the driven set through the shell wall.

Field consideration

Both sealless types depend on the pumped liquid for internal cooling and lubrication. Dry running, or operation at very low flow where the internal circulation collapses, destroys them quickly. They also tolerate solids and entrained gas poorly. The containment shell is the last pressure boundary, so its integrity monitoring matters as much as a seal plan does on a conventional pump.

Design standards and which one to call for

Standards exist to give a common floor for design, dimensions and testing. Service criticality normally decides which one applies.

Do not over-specify

Calling for API 610 on a duty that does not need it buys cost, lead time and spares complexity without buying reliability. Conversely, a B73.1 pump on a hazardous high-energy hydrocarbon service is a false economy. The specification decision follows from the criticality assessment, not from habit.

Application-driven variants

Beyond the construction axes, pumps are built differently for the service they will see. The common application families are petroleum and chemical process, electric and nuclear power, water and wastewater, cooling tower, pulp and paper, slurry, pipeline and water-flood (injection), and high-speed pumps. Each brings its own material selection, impeller type, seal plan and testing expectations — the detail of which runs through the rest of this knowledge base.

Where pumps are used

For context on how broad the field is: water supply, drainage, sewage, irrigation, chemical and petroleum processing, pharmaceutical and medical (including the artificial heart), steel mills, construction dewatering and bypass pumping, and mining — tailings, dust control, groundwater control and site dewatering. Pumps also move whole potatoes, peel hazelnuts in chocolate manufacture, and drive waterjet cutting where a flame torch would be unsafe.
