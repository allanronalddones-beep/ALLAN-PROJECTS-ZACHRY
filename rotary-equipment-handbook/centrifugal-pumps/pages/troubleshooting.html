Field Troubleshooting Guide

Sixteen symptoms an operator or technician actually reports, each with its credible cause list and the checks that discriminate between them. Filter by symptom group or search the full text.

How to use this guide

Find the symptom as it was reported to you, not the component you suspect. Each entry lists the credible causes, grouped so that the cheap, reversible checks come first. Work down the list — do not jump to the expensive cause because it is the interesting one.

Before anything else: establish whether the problem is in the pump or in the system. More pumps are stripped down for a system fault than for a pump fault. See diagnosis before removal.

No symptoms match that filter.

01Pump develops no head pressure and delivers no liquid

The pump is turning but nothing is happening at all. This is a short list because only a few things produce complete failure, and most are mechanical disconnections rather than hydraulic faults.

Check first — no dismantling

  • Pump not primed — not full of liquid, air locked. By far the most common cause. Stop, re-prime, vent the casing, restart.
  • Drive coupling — check it is intact and engaged.

Mechanical disconnection in the rotor

  • Shaft sheared.
  • Impeller key sheared or omitted at assembly.
  • Impeller not installed. It happens — particularly after a rushed overhaul.
02Pump delivers no liquid but develops some pressure

Pressure at the discharge gauge but no delivery means the pump is working on something — usually a trapped pocket of liquid or gas — or the system is asking for more head than the pump can produce.

Suction path blocked or air locked

  • Air lock in the pump casing or in the pipelines.
  • Foot valve malfunctioning — frozen or clogged.
  • Strainer restricted with a build-up of stringy matter.
  • Strainer element restricted with solid deposits such as sand.
  • Suction line clogged.
  • Available NPSH inadequate.
  • Excessive gas or air entrained in the pumped liquid.

The pump cannot meet the system

  • Discharge pressure required by the system is higher than the maximum the pump can develop. Compare the system curve against the pump's shut-off head.
  • Operating speed too low.
  • Wrong direction of rotation. Check before assuming anything else — a reversed pump still delivers, but at substantially reduced head and flow.
  • Undersized impeller, or one machined down to a smaller diameter than required.
03Pump output is below the manufacturer's performance curve

The longest cause list in pump troubleshooting, because almost everything degrades output. Work the groups in order — instruments first, because a measurement error costs nothing to rule out and an overhaul costs a great deal.

1 · Measurement

  • Measuring instruments inaccurate — not properly calibrated or incorrectly installed. Rule this out before anything else.

2 · Air and priming

  • Air enters the pump during operation, or the system was not de-aerated / primed properly before starting.
  • Excessive air or gas entrained in the pumped liquid.

3 · Speed and rotation

  • Verify pump rpm.
  • Incorrect direction of rotation.

4 · The system, not the pump

  • System resistance higher than the pump develops.
  • Pump operating too far out on the head–capacity curve — incorrect selection.
  • Obstruction to flow in suction or discharge piping.
  • Incorrect layout of suction or discharge piping.
  • Incorrect sump suction layout.
  • Liquid level in the suction tank or sump lower than specified.
  • Foot valve clogged or jammed.
  • Suction strainer fouled, restricting flow and lowering NPSH.
  • In a multi-pump system, operation of one pump may affect the others.
  • NPSH-a too low.

5 · Internal leakage — the quiet efficiency thief

  • Excessive leakage through wear rings or other sealing faces. See why this matters most on low specific speed pumps.
  • Excessive leakage through the stuffing box or seal.
  • Excessive liquid recirculated internally to the stuffing box lantern ring or seal.
  • Excessive leakage through the hydraulic balancing device.

6 · Impeller and hydraulic passages

  • Impeller or casing fouled — partially clogged with solid matter.
  • Incorrectly installed impeller.
  • Fins, burrs or sharp edges in the liquid path.
  • Viscosity of the liquid higher than the pump was designed for. Apply the viscosity corrections before concluding the pump is at fault.
04Pump draws higher amps than specified

The pump is doing more work than intended

  • Speed too high.
  • Oversized impeller installed.
  • Process liquid of higher viscosity or specific gravity than originally quoted. Power scales directly with SG — this alone can explain the whole discrepancy.
  • Total system head either higher or lower than anticipated. Lower head on an overloading (low specific speed) pump pushes the duty right and raises power — see operating above BEP.
  • Wrong direction of rotation.

Mechanical drag

  • Misalignment between pump and driver.
  • Rotating parts in contact with stationary parts.
  • Worn or damaged bearings.
  • Bent shaft.
  • Uneven thermal expansion of different parts of the pumping unit.

Sealing drag

  • Packing improperly installed, or incorrect type of packing.
  • Gland too tight.
  • Mechanical seal exerting excessive pressure on the seat.

Lubrication

  • Improper lubrication of bearings, or too much lubricant in them.

Instrumentation

  • Faulty power-measuring instruments, or instruments incorrectly mounted or connected.
05Performance compromised but nothing appears wrong with the system
Read this before dismantling anything

This symptom is usually due to incorrect testing. When the indicators disagree with each other and the machine seems healthy, suspect the measurement chain — not the pump.

Instrument selection and installation

  • Incorrect measuring instruments.
  • Instruments damaged during installation.
  • Instruments mounted in the wrong locations.

Impulse tubing

  • Tubing from pipelines to instruments clogged.
  • Tubing that should be full of liquid not completely de-aerated.
  • Tubing that should be full of air contains some liquid (manometers).
  • Leakage in the tubing or its fittings.
  • Burrs or fins at the mouth of connections between tubing and piping.

Electrical measurement

  • Incorrect wiring connections to electrical instruments.
  • Loose connections at wire terminals.
  • Fouled or oxidised terminals or contacts.

Torque / dynamometer measurement

  • Dust or dirt in the torque bar; torque bar incorrectly mounted.
  • Misalignment or fouled bearings in a dynamometer producing false readings.
  • Excessive friction in the pivots or pulleys guiding the levers and cables.
  • Weight and stiffness of the electrical cables affecting torque readings.

Flow conditions at the tapping

  • Turbulence in the pipelines where instruments are connected.
  • Inner diameter of piping not matching the design specification.
06Pump starts satisfactorily, then performance deteriorates quickly

A short, highly diagnostic list: this pattern is almost always air or gas progressively accumulating, or NPSH falling away once flow establishes.

  • Air leaks into the pump.
  • Pumped liquid contains a high percentage of entrained air or gas.
  • Rate of make-up liquid into the suction sump inadequate, so air is drawn into the pump.
  • An air pocket in the suction line has moved into the pump.
  • Air funnels in the sump suction — vortex formation. Check submergence against the geometry rules.
  • Drop in NPSH.
07Pump operates with noise, vibration, or both

The broadest symptom of all. A frequency analysis will narrow it enormously — unbalance, misalignment and bearing defects each have a distinct signature, and cavitation and vane-pass have their own.

Alignment and mechanical condition

  • Misalignment between pump and driver.
  • Misalignment of pump parts.
  • Rotating parts rubbing against stationary parts.
  • Worn bearings; damaged bearings; improper installation of bearings; improper lubrication of bearings.
  • Bent shaft.
  • Loose bolts.
  • Uneven thermal expansion.

Balance and rotor

  • Rotating elements not balanced.
  • Impeller or casing partially filled with solid matter — out of balance.
  • Damaged impeller; impeller incorrectly mounted.
  • Impeller bore not concentric with its outer diameter, or not square with its face.
  • Excessive radial forces on rotating parts.
  • Pump operating at critical speed.

Hydraulic — cavitation and recirculation

  • Available NPSH too low.
  • Fins, burrs or sharp edges in the waterways causing cavitation.
  • System requirements too far out on the head–capacity curve.
  • Pump operating at very low flow rates.
  • Total head of the system either higher or lower than expected.
  • Excessive air or gas entrained in the liquid.
  • Waterways of impeller or casing badly eroded or rough.
  • Cavitation in the pipelines.
  • Air enters the pump during operation.

Vane pass — the impeller / cutwater gap

  • Too small a distance between impeller outer diameter and volute tongue. This excites vane pass frequency = number of vanes × running speed. Correct it via Gap-B — and note that machining the volute vane to achieve the right Gap-B costs no efficiency.
  • Faulty shape of the volute tongue.

Suction system and sump

  • Suction strainer filled with solid matter; strainer covered with fibrous matter.
  • Incorrect layout of the suction sump.
  • Incorrect layout of suction or discharge piping.
  • Undersized suction or discharge piping and fittings causing cavitation somewhere in the system.
  • Obstruction to flow in suction or discharge piping.
  • Mutual interaction of several pumps within one common system.

Resonance and structure

  • Piping imposes strain on the pump.
  • Improperly designed baseplate or foundations.
  • Resonance between pump speed and the natural frequency of the baseplate or foundations.
  • Resonance between operating speed and the natural frequency of the piping.
  • Resonance between operating speed and valve discs; loose valve disc in the system.
08Stuffing box leaks abnormally
Remember what "normal" is

A correctly adjusted gland should leak slightly — that leakage is the packing's lubricant and coolant. The fault here is abnormal leakage.

Packing itself

  • Improperly installed packing; incorrect type of packing.
  • Excessive clearance at the bottom of the stuffing box — between shaft and box bottom.
  • Seal cage improperly located.
  • Dirt or grit in the sealing liquid.
  • Water-seal pipe clogged.
  • Failure to provide cooling liquid to water-cooled stuffing boxes.

Shaft motion at the packing — the real root cause in most cases

  • Worn bearings.
  • Rotating element not balanced.
  • Excessive radial forces on rotating parts — i.e. the pump is being run too far from BEP.
  • Bent shaft; rotating parts running off-centre.
  • Bore of impeller not concentric with its outer diameter, or not square with its face.
  • Misalignment of pump parts.

Shaft surface

  • Shaft sleeve worn or scorched at the packing.
09Gland packing has a short working life

Largely the same cause set as abnormal leakage, plus the installation and lubrication faults specific to short life.

Installation and adjustment

  • Improperly installed packing; incorrect type of packing.
  • Gland too tight. The classic cause — it runs the shaft hot and wears the surface rapidly.
  • Improper lubrication of the packing.
  • Seal cage improperly located in the stuffing box, preventing sealing fluid from entering.
  • Water-seal pipe clogged.
  • Failure to provide cooling liquid to a water-cooled stuffing box.
  • Excessive clearance at the bottom of the stuffing box.
  • The space in the stuffing box where the packing sits is eccentric to the shaft.
  • Dirt or grit in the sealing liquid.

Shaft motion and surface

  • Worn bearings; rotating parts running off-centre from damaged bearings or other parts.
  • Rotating element not balanced; excessive radial forces on rotating parts.
  • Bent shaft.
  • Impeller bore not concentric with its OD, or not square with its face.
  • Misalignment of pump parts.
  • Shaft scorched where it contacts the packing.
10Mechanical seal fails prematurely
Context

Mechanical seals are the prime cause of pump failure. But note how few of the causes below are the seal itself — most are rotor motion or fluid condition. Replacing the seal without addressing those guarantees a repeat.

Rotor motion at the seal

  • Worn bearings.
  • Rotating elements not balanced.
  • Excessive radial forces on rotating parts.
  • Bent shaft. Remember a 0.075 mm shaft runout moves the faces 0.15 mm total.
  • Misalignment of pump parts; rotating elements running off-centre from bearing or other damage.

Geometry of the installation

  • Sealing face not perpendicular to the pump axis. This is the seal housing squareness check — limit 0.05 mm.
  • Misalignment of internal seal parts preventing proper mating between seal and seat.
  • Mechanical seal improperly installed.
  • Incorrect type of mechanical seal.

Fluid at the faces

  • Mechanical seal has been run dry.
  • Dirt or grit in the seal-flushing liquid.
  • Abrasive particles in the liquid coming into contact with the seal.
11Bearings fail prematurely
Baseline expectation

Only about 10% of rolling element bearings reach their 3–5 year design life, and lubricant contamination is the dominant reason. Check the lubrication and sealing group first.

Lubrication and contamination

  • Bearings improperly lubricated; faulty lubrication system.
  • Excessive grease in bearings.
  • Dirt ingress into the bearing housing.
  • Water has entered the bearing housing. 0.002% water in the oil costs about 48% of bearing life.
  • Inadequate cooling of bearings, or of the lubricant, where applicable.
  • Source of cooling media restricted or shut off from the bearing housing.

Load — usually from how the pump is operated

  • Excessive radial loads on rotating parts.
  • Excessive axial loads.
  • Pump operates for prolonged time at low flow rate.
  • Excessive suction pressure.
  • Excessive wear of impeller wear rings, which adversely affects support of the rotating element.

Rotor condition

  • Damaged impeller; impeller partially clogged.
  • Rotating elements not balanced.
  • Bent shaft.
  • Impeller bore not concentric with OD or not square with the hub face.
  • Misalignment of pump parts; misalignment between pump and driver.
  • Rotating parts running off-centre from damaged or misaligned parts.

Installation and housing

  • Improper installation of bearings; improper workmanship during installation.
  • Bores of bearing housing not concentric with bores in the water end.
  • Cracked or damaged bearing housing.
  • Too tight a fit between the line bearing and its seat, which may prevent it sliding under axial load and transfer that load to the line bearing.
  • Improper baseplate or foundations.
12Bearings run noisily — diagnosing by the sound

The character of the noise narrows the cause substantially. Listen before you measure.

A · Steady high-pitch tone

  • Excessive radial load.
  • Excessive axial load.
  • Misalignment.
  • Too much clearance between bearing and shaft, and/or housing.

B · Continuous or intermittent low-pitch tone

  • Bearing brinelled — indentations in the raceway, usually from impact loading during installation or transport.
  • Pitted raceway, from dirt.
  • Resonance with other structural pump parts.

C · Intermittent rattles, rumbles or clicks

  • Loose machine parts.
  • Dirt in the bearings.
  • Clearance between balls and races too large for the application.
  • Bearings that require preloading not adequately preloaded.

D · Intermittent squeal or high-pitch tone

  • Balls skidding from excessive clearance between balls and races.
  • Balls skidding from insufficient preloading, where required.
  • Shaft rubbing against the housing — from improper mounting of the housing, from a bent shaft, or from having been machined eccentrically.
13Pump overheats or seizes
Stop the pump

Overheating toward seizure is a progressive failure. Continued running turns a seal-and-bearing job into a casing-and-impeller job.

No liquid, or not enough of it

  • Pump allowed to run dry.
  • Vapour or air pockets inside the pump.
  • Pump operates near shut-off. Calculate the temperature rise rate — at closed valve the casing contents can reach flashing in minutes.
  • Simultaneous operation of poorly matched pumps — one of them may be sitting at shut-off. See parallel operation with dissimilar curves.

Mechanical contact

  • Internal misalignment from too much pipe strain, poor foundations, or faulty repair work.
  • Internal rubbing of rotating parts against stationary parts.
  • Worn or damaged bearings; poor lubrication.

Material selection

  • Rotating and stationary wear rings made of identical, galling-prone materials. This is why SS 316 is not used for wear rings and why the two rings are deliberately given a 50–100 BHN hardness differential.
14Pump cavitates when NPSH-a is increased
Why this apparent paradox happens

It looks impossible and it is not. The increase in available NPSH has reduced the system resistance so far that the pump now operates far out on the Q–H curve. Out there, internal velocities are high, static head falls toward vapour pressure, and NPSH-r rises — so the margin closes from the other end. See breakaway NPSH-r.

The conditions that produce it:

  • Oversized impeller installed in the pump.
  • Pump operates at excessive speed.
  • Breakdown or serious leak in the discharge line.
  • Open bypass in the discharge line.
  • Extremely large clearances between impeller and casing.
  • A hole in the casing allowing liquid from the pressure side to return to the suction inlet.

Note that the last three are all internal short circuits — the pump is working against far less head than you think it is.

15Safe operation — the do's
  • Read the manufacturer's instructions before operation.
  • Check that sumps and piping are clean.
  • Prime the pump before starting.
  • Regulate flow, if necessary, with the discharge valve.
  • A pump may be run up with the discharge valve closed only for a limited time.
  • Never over-tighten packing glands.
  • Check alignment before placing a pump in service.
  • Operate within the manufacturer's speed recommendations.
  • Support piping adequately to avoid stresses on pump casings.
  • Allow the pump foundation to set firmly before bolting down the pump.
  • Ensure NPSH-a exceeds NPSH-r by a margin — this list cites 0.5–1.0 m; most specifications call for 1.0–1.5 m or the applicable ratio, which is the figure to work to.
  • Ensure proper pipe gradients and layout to avoid trapping air in suction lines — air lock.
  • Guard pump drives and all moving or high-temperature parts.
16Safe operation — the don'ts
  • Don't fail to read the manufacturer's instructions.
  • Don't fail to check that sumps and piping are clean.
  • Don't run a pump without liquid.
  • Don't regulate flow with a suction valve. Throttling the suction destroys NPSH-a directly and is the fastest way to cavitate a healthy pump.
  • Don't run a pump with a closed discharge (beyond the brief start-up period).
  • Don't over-tighten packing glands.
  • Don't fail to check alignment.
  • Don't exceed the manufacturer's speed recommendations.
  • Don't impose piping stresses on pump casings.
  • Don't bolt a pump to a foundation until it is firmly set.
  • Don't attempt a suction lift greater than the safety margin under NPSH-a allows.
  • Don't trap air in suction lines.
  • Don't allow pumps to run with unguarded moving or high-temperature parts.

The pattern worth noticing

Look at the top six rows. Almost every mechanically-presenting pump complaint traces back to the same small cluster: misalignment, bearing condition, shaft straightness, balance, and radial load from running off BEP — and pipe strain causes or worsens most of them.

That is why the flange disconnection check and the 70–120% operating band deliver more reliability per unit of effort than any other two things in this knowledge base.
