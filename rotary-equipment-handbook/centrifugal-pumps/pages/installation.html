Chapter 08 · Project lifecycle

Installation and Commissioning

Siting, foundation design, grouting, alignment, suction and discharge piping rules, pre-operational checks and the start-up sequence — the stage where most of a pump's service life is won or lost.

After specification, selection, sizing, inspection and testing, installation is the next key factor in reliable operation. Properly installed, operated and maintained, a pump can give many years of trouble-free service. Incorrectly installed, maintenance and operational problems will impede its performance for its entire life — and no amount of later maintenance recovers what a bad installation costs.

Installation needs careful preparation and planning, coordinated between the supervising engineer, the mechanical and electrical contractors, and the suppliers. It is a series of steps, and each one has to be right before the next begins.

Site location

A pre-installation activity. Before the equipment even arrives, layout drawings are available, which allows the site location to be selected and marked out.

Ergonomics is a prime factor. Where equipment is accessible for maintenance, technicians perform better and operators activate and control it more efficiently. A pump or motor that is difficult to access and maintain becomes a cause of longer downtime and lower availability. Poor workmanship caused by difficult access leads directly to lower reliability.

Safety is the main aspect

A difficult site has a higher probability of accidents. Site selection deserves considerable attention and should be done by someone with real practical experience in pump operation and maintenance — not solely from a layout drawing.

Receipt and physical inspection

Examine all pumps and auxiliary equipment on receipt for signs of apparent damage, and notify any damage found. Where installation is not immediate, store in a clean, dry location protected from damage, following the manufacturer's recommendations and protecting from environmental extremes.

Check the equipment before storage to anticipate problems at installation time — check sizes, the design features called for on the plans and specifications, and all interface components. These simple early checks minimise installation problems considerably.

Recommended practices on receipt and in storage
OpeningsKeep all nozzles and openings covered or plugged until the piping is attached.
BearingsFill bearing housings with oil of the recommended viscosity. For greased bearings, pump in new grease and displace the old.
PreservationCoat all exposed surfaces with rust preventive. For storage beyond 6 months, coat the internals too with a suitable rust preventive or oil mist.
PackingRemove pump packings with sleeves.
Mechanical sealsHandle carefully. Do not subject to impact or excessive vibration.

Pre-alignment checks

If installing immediately after receipt, carry out an alignment check of the pump with its motor on the baseplate, to confirm that the final alignment tolerances in the specification are actually achievable. Use a reverse dial indicator or laser alignment method.

Why do this before anything is grouted

If the specified alignment is not achievable, there is still time to rectify the fault — which saves time and money and prevents the quick fixes that get applied when the problem is discovered at the final stage of installation.

Once correct alignment is confirmed, the pump and motor can be removed from the base to ease installation and prevent damage to critical components.

Pump foundation

Locating the foundation

Once the site location is fixed, locating the foundation is largely fine-tuning to bring it into line with existing equipment, piping and vessels. The site-location factors still apply: allow room for walkways, existing and new piping, operator and technician accessibility, and aesthetics.

What can usually be altered is the orientation of the pump's face, its proximity to walls, and the height and depth of the foundation.

Changing pump height changes the hydraulics

If pump height or location is altered, ensure the operating parameters can still be met — and the prime consideration is the NPSH margin or ratio. Raising a pump 600 mm to clear a walkway can quietly consume the whole suction margin. Where installation standards or guidelines already exist, obtain the responsible engineer's consent.

Design and dimensions

The foundation has two specific purposes:

  1. to support the pump so it operates safely;
  2. its mass damps the pump vibrations.

It must provide enough rigidity to absorb the axial, transverse and torsional loads the rotating pump imposes. Proper structural design requires an evaluation of soil conditions so that both dynamic and static forces are considered.

Foundation design requirements
MassAt least three times the total weight of supported equipment.
IsolationRests on solid or stabilised earth, completely independent of other foundations, pads, walls or operating platforms.
ConcreteA minimum of 3000 psi steel-reinforced concrete.
ResonanceResonant frequency must not be excitable by pump operating speed or multiples of it.
Common baseAll units — pump, gearbox and motor — rest on a common foundation.
ThermalDesigned for uniform temperatures, to minimise distortion and misalignment.
SeismicDesigned accounting for regional seismic activity.

Rules of thumb for dimensions

  • The 30° rule. Drop two lines from the pump centre at 30° to the vertical. The width of the foundation should exceed their spread.
  • Mass. Foundation weight at least three times the mass of supported equipment.
  • Edge margin. For pumps under 500 hp, at least 3 in between baseplate edge and foundation edge, all the way around. For higher horsepower, 6 in.

Excavation and forms

With location, orientation and dimensions fixed, mark the location and excavate a correctly sized cavity to the correct depth. Typical requirements: cement saw, jackhammer, air supply, wheelbarrow, shovels, a small front-end loader, a disposal method for excavated material, and digging permits. Excavation for an average-sized centrifugal pump takes a maximum of about two days.

Then place the forms — reusable or single use. The forms must be true, solid, well braced and liquid-tight. Leak tightness matters particularly because epoxies seep through small holes far more readily than cement.

Once installed, ensure the forms are level, square and securely fastened to the floor or ground — the weight of cement and epoxy bears on them and can easily cause a shift. If the form shifts during pouring, the job has to be terminated and restarted.

For epoxy pours, coat the insides of the forms with an anti-stick material so the forms can be removed without destroying them. Wax is a good anti-stick material: it coats well and the forms can be coated before assembly and installation.

Rebar and anchor bolts

Install the rebar and pump hold-down bolts. Rebar ring and post count depends on the foundation dimensions — a 60″ × 60″ × 36″ foundation typically takes three rings of rebar tied to eight posts. Rebar is placed several inches from the sides, top and bottom, equally spaced from top and bottom.

Then the anchor bolts. Length is typically 10 to 15 times the bolt diameter, which is what gives the bolt enough stretch to develop the design holding force.

The anchor bolt failure nobody expects

If epoxy grout is allowed to grip the anchor bolt, the bolt will break at the grout surface even when tightened only to the design torque — because it cannot stretch. This requirement is met at foundation design stage by using bolt sleeves in the concrete.

Where sleeves are used, fill them with non-binding material — sand, flexible foam or wax — to prevent epoxy bonding to the bolt. The exposed length from the top of the concrete to the bottom of the baseplate can also be wrapped with one layer of weather stripping and one layer of duct tape.

Typical ANSI pump anchor bolt detail

An acceptable hold-down bolt for most ANSI pumps is a 5/8 in J-bolt mounted in a 1½ in pipe, the pipe at least 6 in long:

  • the J-bolt extends past the top of the pipe by at least the pump base height plus 1½ in;
  • it extends past the bottom of the pipe by an inch or two before the "J" bends;
  • a washer is welded to the bottom of the pipe, the J-bolt passes through it, and the J-bolt is then welded to the washer.

The bolts must be true to themselves and with respect to each other, which is best achieved with a single bracket securing all the bolts.

Pouring

Concrete pour

Pour the concrete mix slowly while stirring the cement — normally from a cement truck, or pumped or wheelbarrowed where a truck cannot reach. Trowel-finish the top of the pad when the cement is ready.

Curing must complete before epoxy grout

The epoxy–concrete bond is sensitive to moisture, which must be prevented at all times. Curing of freshly poured concrete must complete before epoxy grout is applied.

Proper method: run the ASTM 157-80 concrete shrinkage test to determine when shrinkage drops to minimum — that indicates the end of the chemical reaction between cement and water.

Rule of thumb in its absence: standard concrete (5-bag mix) — 28 days; quick-setting concrete (6–7 bag mix) — 7 days.

Field check: tape one square foot of plastic sheet over the concrete block and leave overnight. Moisture on the underside means the concrete is not ready. Repeat until no moisture appears.

Removing laitance

Surface preparation begins two to three days after the pour. It may be necessary to chip the top ½″ to 1″ from the foundation surface to remove the cement-rich surface layer called laitance — a weak surface created when concrete is cast, which would not provide proper adhesion or support for the grout beneath the baseplate.

Sand blasting removes laitance and exposes the aggregate. The most common method is to wait for the concrete to cure and then chip the laitance with light-duty pneumatic hammers. Jackhammers and sharp pointed chisels should not be used — they damage the sound concrete beneath.

Chamfer all foundation edges at least 2 to 4 in at 45° to remove stress concentration. Remove all dust, dirt, chips, oil and water, and cover the foundation.

Full epoxy foundation

It is sometimes necessary or advantageous to make the entire foundation from epoxy. Initial cost is higher, but it offers:

  • superior vibration damping;
  • better chemical resistance;
  • faster curing — epoxy cures in 24 hours, against 7 days minimum even for quick-setting cement, so equipment installation can begin almost immediately.

Procedure: mix epoxy parts A (resin) and B (hardener) per instructions. Mix both parts completely and leave nothing unmixed — unmixed parts are a hazard needing special disposal, whereas the mixture of the two is not hazardous.

Add the mixed resin to an empty mortar mixer, then add the aggregate: pure silica, some with sand texture and some with pea-gravel texture, at a specific ratio set by ambient conditions. Pure silica is used instead of sand and pea gravel for its superior heat-sink capability, and it adds to the overall strength.

Mix to uniform consistency, slowly, so no air is entrained — air is detrimental to pour strength. A spiral-blade mortar mixer is best suited. Pour into the hole and repeat until complete. An average-size foundation can be poured in under 4 hours when properly administered. Complete any finishing touches before the epoxy cures.

Baseplate and sole plate preparation

Remove all equipment from the baseplate or sole plate before grouting. This helps level the plate and reduces unwanted distortion. The pump and motor/turbine/engine are mounted after the baseplate has been properly grouted.

Baseplate preparation checklist
Grout-contact surface Coat with inorganic zinc silicate or a compatible primer. The surface should be bare or rusted, free of blisters, and not smooth — a smooth surface will not bond properly with epoxy grout.
Grout openings At least one grouting opening in each bulkhead section and/or each 12 ft² of base area as a minimum.
Vent holes At the corners of each bulkhead compartment, to ensure no voids form from trapped air. Typically ½ in diameter.
Corners Round all baseplate corners to 20 in radius. Epoxy shrinks as it cures; rounding prevents stress corrosion in the grout. Sharp corners eventually crack the grout.
Cleanliness Free from oil, grease and rust before placing on the foundation.

Levelling

Rest the baseplate on the foundation supported on levelling screws, rectangular levelling shims or taper wedges placed close to the foundation bolts to prevent distortion. Coat levelling screws adequately with grease or wax to prevent epoxy adhesion.

Baseplate level tolerance
0.002 in per foot
Min. shim under driver
1/8 in
Machined surface overhang
0.1 in beyond feet

Level side-to-side, end-to-end and diagonally to within 0.002 in per foot. Machined surfaces must be flat and parallel, and the mounting surface tolerance must remain the same after the anchor bolts are tightened. Once level, confirm all wedges and shims are in contact with both baseplate and foundation, tighten the foundation bolts evenly, and re-check the levels.

Before the grout is poured — four things that are hard to fix later

  1. Check the elevation of the machined surfaces to confirm it allows a minimum 1/8 in shim thickness under the driving equipment.
  2. Ensure eight alignment positioning screws are provided for positioning the driver. Machined mounting surfaces should extend 0.1 in beyond the pump and driver feet on all sides.
  3. Drill and tap two holes on the baseplate flanges each side of the anchor-bolt holes to make provision for 1½ in jack/levelling screws.
  4. Grease and insert the coupling guard bolts into the baseplate. It is difficult to drill and tap those holes once they are filled with epoxy grout.

Grouting

Grout is a hardenable material — mortar, concrete or epoxy — placed under and around the baseplate to ensure intimate contact with the foundation. The reasons for grouting:

  • to provide a uniformly distributed load-bearing surface;
  • to provide effective damping of machinery vibration;
  • to fill cavities and cover projections, eliminating unsafe conditions and improving performance.

Two types are in use: epoxy grout (resin, hardener and aggregate), and cement plus a natural or metallic aggregate.

Epoxy grout

Forms. Lay out heavy-duty forms — epoxy weighs nearly 2.5 times as much as concrete. Use ¾ in plywood with adequate bracing. Wax-coat the surfaces that will contact the epoxy, normally three coats with sufficient interval between coats for wax penetration and drying. Give the forms a 1 in, 45° chamfer on the vertical and horizontal edges so mating allows minimum seepage; use a plastic sealant at all joints and at the foundation interface if needed.

One pour or two. API-design baseplates require two-pour grouts: one to fill the void between concrete and baseplate flanges, and one to fill the void between the baseplate flange and the top of the baseplate. However, if the free surface of the grout at the baseplate flanges is confined 6–7 in higher, the grout level at the top of the baseplate can be filled in one pour. A one-pour grout can be accomplished in 45 minutes and, with curing complete in 24 hours, the forms can be removed — and where ambient temperature is above 25 °C, the pump and driver can be installed as soon as the forms come off. A two-pour job needs more time and cost.

Temperature window. Epoxy grouts have a narrow range for mixing and placement: 10 °C to 35 °C for best life, flowability and curing. Below that, add low-temperature accelerators and keep the foundation and baseplate heated. Above it, place temporary shades over the baseplate 24 hours before and 48 hours after the pour.

Mixing. Mix in a wheelbarrow with a mortar-mixing hoe, or in a motorised mixer where the grout area is larger (10 units or more) — keeping blade speed limited to 15 rpm. For hand mixing use two wheelbarrows to keep a continuous supply to the men pouring. Either way, mix slowly to prevent froth or air entrapment, for 3–5 minutes after adding hardener to resin. Record the timing of mixing and pouring and confirm it meets the specification.

Pouring. Pour slowly using a large funnel placed about a metre above the baseplate, to provide the force needed to push grout out of the vent holes. A positive displacement pump can perform the same function. As grout overflows from a vent hole, cover it with duct tape and continue filling. Take random grout samples with ambient temperature and location recorded.

Finishing. As the epoxy begins to harden, form domes so no water accumulates in low areas; remove after 24 hours. Relieve the jackscrews after 3 days. After full cure, remove the sealing tape and the wedges or shims, and fill the shim holes with silicone caulking. Then tighten the anchor bolts to the recommended torque.

The hammer test for voids

Check the grout job by ringing the baseplate with a hammer. A good job sounds like hitting a lead plate; one with voids rings like a bell. This takes two minutes and finds the defect that would otherwise show up as unexplained vibration after commissioning. Any voids detected should be pressure grouted.

Cement grout

The procedure is similar to epoxy. Lay out the forms with the same precautions. Where the foundation is concrete, keep the top surface saturated with water for the period recommended by the grout manufacturer, then remove that water from the top and from boltholes just before placing the grout.

Prevent grout entering the anchor bolt sleeves: fill sleeves with a non-bonding pliable material such as asphalt or silicone rubber moulding compound, to prevent a water pocket around the bolt. Wrap duct tape around the exposed anchor bolt threads to prevent direct grout contact.

Temperature requirements are similar to epoxy, but the range must be maintained for a minimum of 24 hours after pouring. Prepare the grout per instructions with the right amount and quality of oil-free water.

Placement must be rapid and continuous. Start at one end of the baseplate and work toward the other, to ensure all air is positively vented and no pockets are trapped.

Cement grout finishing levels
Grout cut backTo the bottom outer edge of the baseplate or sole plate, tapered to the existing concrete.
Flange-type support baseplatesTop of grout at the top of the flange.
Solid-sided baseplates and sole platesTop of grout 1 in above the bottom of the baseplate or underside of the sole plate.
Outside top edgesChamfered at 45°.

After initial set, trim to the levels shown on the drawings. After complete cure, hammer test for air voids and pressure grout any found. Forms can come out after 24 hours. Levelling shims and wedges may be removed after cure, filling the voids with grout without aggregate.

Remove the levelling screws

Where levelling screws were used, remove them after the grout has cured so the full equipment weight is distributed evenly over the grouted area, then caulk the holes with putty. Leaving them in place means the machine is standing on a few point loads rather than on the grout bed — which defeats the purpose of grouting.

Finally, tighten the anchor bolts to the right torque. The pump and driver are now ready for installation.

Installing the pump and driver

Once the grout is cured and the base is clean, remove the bolts used to seal the boltholes and install the pump — which should be in operation-ready condition when it goes on the base.

Place the driver, motor or turbine, at the location shown on the drawings and confirm the distance between shaft ends (DBSE):

  • set DBSE with the pump and driver shafts pulled toward each other;
  • for motor drives with sleeve bearings, set DBSE with the motor shaft at its magnetic centre.
Check rotation before coupling up

For an electric motor, confirm the motor is wired correctly for the correct direction of rotation. This check must be carried out before the equipment is coupled up. Pumps, seals and magnetic drive bearings can be ruined if operated dry or in reverse.

After the rotation check, de-energise the motor and lock out the breaker.

Final alignment

Align pump and motor to the final tolerances using a reverse dial gauge or a laser alignment tool.

Soft foot

This is the stage at which a soft foot condition manifests. Loosen the hold-down bolts one at a time and use a dial gauge to record movement between the machine foot and the baseplate or sole plate.

Soft foot limit
0.025 mm (1 mil)

Any movement in excess of 1 mil indicates soft foot and must be corrected by adding the required amount of shims under the feet.

Thermal growth with a steam turbine driver

Alignment with a steam turbine is usually carried out at ambient conditions. When steam is introduced the turbine centreline rises, producing misalignment. Compute the vertical growth and offset for it:

Rise = 1.2 mils per inch of height from base to centreline, per 100 °C rise
(equivalently 1.2 mm per metre of height, per 100 °C rise)

Example: an impulse steam turbine with 130 °C exhaust in a 30 °C ambient is a 100 °C rise. With 12 in from foot base to shaft centreline, the thermal growth is 14.4 mils.

Then bolt the piping — and re-check

After alignment is complete, bolt up the piping associated with the pump and steam turbine. Then check the alignment again; it should give similar readings.

This is the check that catches pipe strain

If the alignment readings have changed after bolting the piping, investigate the piping and make suitable corrections. Left unattended, this causes stress on the pump casing and nozzles — and it is the root cause behind a great many "unexplained" bearing and seal failures months later.

Once alignment is approved, drill the support pads for the pumps and drivers at two locations for taper dowels. Locate the dowels preferably at the end carrying the thrust bearings.

Associated piping and fittings

Piping associated with the pump must be anchored and supported independently of the pump. Without adequate anchorage, expansion and contraction of the line transfers forces to the pump casing. Where pipes are not supported, their weight is borne by the pump casing and nozzles, causing them to deflect and crack — and the pump's seal life suffers from the same strain.

Connections must be carefully aligned axially, angularly and in length, and the flange boltholes must be in phase with the pump nozzle holes.

The one-minute test for pipe strain

Disconnect all the suction and discharge flanges on the pump. If levers are required to force the pipe flange onto the pump nozzles to facilitate bolting, you can be certain the pump will sooner or later start giving bearing and other problems.

This is the single most valuable check in this chapter. It costs nothing and it finds the defect that no amount of subsequent alignment, balancing or bearing replacement will cure.

Suction (inlet) piping

Suction piping rules
Support Piping run and connection fittings properly aligned and supported separately to reduce strain on the casing.
Straight run Straight run leading to the suction nozzle at least 3 to 6 pipe diameters from the upstream elbow.
Elbow type Standard type or long radius.
Negative suction All suction piping must be airtight.
Pipe size At least one commercial size larger than the pump inlet opening.
Reducer Eccentric reducer with the flat side on top — so air cannot be trapped in the line. Straight length after the eccentric reducer: 2 pipe diameters.
Velocity Sized so liquid velocity is not more than 2 to 3 m/s.
Slope In negative suction, a continuous rise to the suction inlet; minimum slope 6 mm per 100 mm. Not required in flooded suction.
Isolation valves In negative suction, no isolation valves recommended; they may be provided in flooded suction. Even fully open, a valve contributes friction loss and lowers available NPSH — and on a pump with high negative suction lift, NPSH-a is already low.
Drain and gauge Provide a means to drain the contents between the suction isolation valves and the pump casing, and a tapping for a suction pressure gauge.

Sump, submergence and vortexing

Submergence and strainer geometry in negative suction
Strainer submergence Minimum depth at least 3 pipe diameters, measured from the upper row of holes of the strainer.
Strainer to floor Distance between the bottom of the strainer and the sump floor: 2 pipe diameters.
Bellmouth / funnel With D = 2d, the optimum distance between the bellmouth rim and the sump bottom is about 0.5 d. Larger leads to eddies and vortices.
Minimum submergence At least 2 d.
Strainer area and mesh Suction strainer at least 4 × the suction pipe area; mesh size must screen out solids that could clog the impeller.
Why vortexing matters

Swirling vortices draw air into the suction pipe, which interferes with pump performance — and it presents as a baffling intermittent problem because it depends on sump level. The geometry rules above exist to prevent it; they are cheap at design stage and almost impossible to fix afterwards.

Discharge piping

  • Piping run and fittings properly aligned and supported separately.
  • Discharge pipe size at least one commercial size larger than the pump outlet.
  • Minimise the number of fittings and size changes to prevent fluid friction losses.
  • Check valve of the non-slam type, to prevent hydraulic shock.
  • Isolation valve downstream of the check valve, so the check valve can be taken out for servicing.
  • Concentric reducers in the discharge, to minimise friction loss. (Contrast the suction, which takes an eccentric reducer.)
  • A pressure tapping as close as possible to the pump outlet and before the isolation valve, to measure shut-off head.
  • A second pressure tapping downstream of the reducer, as a good indicator of operating pressure.
  • Expansion joints only after careful piping analysis, especially where discharge pressures are high.

On-site installation and commissioning

Once the shop tests are witnessed and verified, the pump is disconnected and transported to site for installation and commissioning.

Expect the duty point to differ — and know why

Once installed, checked, primed and tested, the pump may or may not operate at the design point as calculated. There are several legitimate reasons:

  • "As designed" versus "as constructed." Differences in pipe runs, number of bends, or quality of fittings change the frictional resistance the pump must overcome — up or down — moving the operating point away from the design point.
  • Over-generous friction allowance at design stage. Too much allowance for Hf means the pump selected is oversized, and it will operate off the performance curve or far to the right of BEP.
  • Change in fluid properties.
  • Anomalies introduced between shop test and site — wrong impeller size, wrong pump speed, incorrect direction of rotation.
Commissioning acceptance of the system resistance evaluation
Measured deviation from design pointVerdict on the total system resistance Hts
Within ±2.5%Good The evaluation of Hts is considered good.
Within ±5%Satisfactory The evaluation is considered satisfactory.
Greater than 5%Investigate The system needs to be checked out.

It is always good practice to have the pump manufacturer's representative involved at installation, testing and commissioning.

Commissioning test logs, similar to those drawn up at the shop test, must be filled in and held on record as part of the offer and acceptance protocol. Based on the operating point at commissioning, the system resistance curve must be developed and drawn up for the pump and system. These documents are what you will refer to later when verifying pump and system performance — they are the baseline for every performance check in Chapter 09.

Pre-operational checks

Mainly focused on the auxiliary systems.

Pre-operational checklist
Double mechanical seals (back-to-back, external pressurised sealant) Flush and clean the lines before connecting them to the pump. If the casing is pressurised, check the sealant supply pressure is 1–1.5 kg/cm² above casing pressure — lower pressure can cause the inner seal to open up and contaminate the process fluid.
Tandem mechanical seals The overhead reservoir providing thermosyphon circulation for the outboard seal must be thoroughly cleaned by oil flush before connecting to the pump.
Cooling water Flush all cooling water lines to pumps and turbines. Confirm supply and return lines are connected to the correct headers.
Bearing lubrication Drain bearing housings of their oil and refill with fresh oil of correct viscosity. Verify oil reaches all bearings.
Oil mist systems Must be up and running for almost 12 hours before start-up. Oil mist piping must slope toward the equipment with no sags or low spots.
Re-confirm Direction of rotation of the motor · over-speed trip setting of a steam turbine · greasing of the coupling · earthing of equipment.
Separate lube oil system Where the pump and turbine have one, all alarms and trips must be checked and tested.
Final mechanical Rotate the pump by hand and look for any rubs. Place the coupling guard and tighten the bolts.
Electrical Inform the electrical department to energise.

Preparation for start-up

The sequence from here:

  1. Open the pump suction valve slowly and check all joints for leakage.
  2. Open the pump casing to vent any vapour. This can be tricky with flashing hydrocarbons, so it has to be done several times.
  3. If the pumping temperature is high, allow the pump to warm up. For multistage pumps with long rotors, it is a good idea to keep rotating the rotor 180° every 30 minutes while warming — otherwise the rotor sags and bows.
  4. Open the sealant and cooling water lines and ensure circulation.
  5. Discharge valve position depends on the pump type: kept closed for low specific speed pumps, open for higher specific speed pumps. This prevents overloading the motor drive — see Chapter 05.
  6. Confirm the electrical supply has been energised.
  7. Start the pump.

Pump in operation

First-run checks and their acceptance
Discharge pressure Check it is along expected lines. If the pressure does not come up, the most probable reason is improper priming — stop the pump and re-prime. On a low specific speed pump, the discharge pressure falls as the discharge valve is opened, which is normal.
Flow rate Confirm.
Vibration Take measurements of the entire train with a data collector. Record overall and filtered readings, and record and store the frequency plots. Study them for possible defects. Because overall vibration varies with the operating point, record readings and frequency plots at 4 or 5 operating points including the normal and rated points — this is your vibration baseline for the life of the machine.
Seal leakage Confirm. Some leakage in the initial stages is possible and may settle down after wear-in.
Bearing temperature Pump and motor/turbine bearings no more than 10–20 °C above ambient. Higher indicates a bearing in distress — unless they are greased, in which case the most probable cause is over-greasing. A vibration or shock pulse analysis confirms it; if so, wait 24 hours and allow the excess grease to work out.
Performance check Once steady state is achieved, carry out a performance check and determine the efficiency. Plot it on the pump characteristic curves — this is the commissioning baseline for all future performance monitoring.
Shutdown — and a free test

When the trial is complete, shut the discharge valve partially and switch off the motor. Then keep an eye on any reverse rotation of the pump. That observation is a free functional check of the non-return valve: if the pump turns backward, the NRV is passing. Given what the reverse rotation quadrant can do to a machine, it is worth the thirty seconds.
