Chapter 09 · Project lifecycle

Maintenance and Reliability

Condition monitoring and overhaul: the four-pillar predictive programme, failure diagnosis before strip-down, the dimensional checks that matter, reassembly of single-stage, vertical and multistage pumps, and the economics of when to overhaul.

The basis of centrifugal pump maintenance is a direct function of criticality. A garden water pump does not require the attention of a boiler feed water pump in a power plant or a firewater pump in a refinery. So the first task is not technical — it is to classify the machine.

Criticality and maintenance philosophy

A pump is critical where:

  • failure can affect plant safety;
  • it is essential for plant operation and a shutdown will curtail process throughput;
  • there is no standby or installed spare;
  • it is a large horsepower pump;
  • capital cost is high and it is expensive to repair, or has a long repair lead time;
  • it is a perennial "bad actor" — a pump that wrecks on the slightest provocation of an off-duty operation;
  • it is part of a pump train where better operation could save energy or improve yields.
Classification and the maintenance philosophy that follows
ClassMaintenance philosophy
CriticalPredictive and proactive techniques
EssentialPreventive maintenance
General purposeLess stringent — may be run to failure

In practice a mix-and-match of techniques is applied, with the prime intention of maximising runtime lengths and reducing downtime and costs. Current focus for continuous process plant pumps is a blend of Predictive and Preventive Maintenance (PPM).

The four pillars of a PPM programme

Individually each gives an indication of pump condition. Collectively they give the complete picture. Running only one or two is the commonest reason condition monitoring programmes fail to predict anything.

  1. Performance monitoring
  2. Vibration monitoring
  3. Oil and particle analysis
  4. System analysis — the one most often omitted

Performance monitoring

Six parameters tell you how the pump is performing:

  1. suction pressure, Ps
  2. discharge pressure, Pd
  3. flow, Q
  4. pump speed, N
  5. pump efficiency, η
  6. power

With an electric motor driver, power is the easiest to measure with portable instruments clipped onto the phase cables, giving amps, volts, power factor and hence power. Combined with the process parameters, efficiency can be computed and benchmarked against the OEM's expected values — see the procedure in Chapter 03.

Suction and discharge pressures are measured using calibrated pressure gauges screwed onto the fittings provided on the pipelines with isolation valves.

Flow measurement in the field

Normally done with obtrusive instruments — venturi tubes, orifice plates, pitot tubes. Where none are installed, non-obtrusive doppler or transit-time devices can be used, and these can be portable.

Caution on clamp-on flow meters

Each device must be calibrated, and independent testing has shown these devices are sensitive to the liquid being pumped and are not precisely accurate. Treat a clamp-on reading as an indication, and be careful about declaring a pump degraded on its evidence alone.

Analysis of efficiency — or inefficiency — helps determine whether the losses are hydraulic, internal recirculation, or mechanical.

Thermodynamic efficiency monitoring

Direct-reading thermodynamic pump efficiency monitors (the Yates meter and similar) interpret operating efficiency dynamically by measuring and computing the temperature rise of the fluid — in millikelvin — as it moves from suction to discharge. Highly sensitive temperature probes and pressure transducers are positioned on the suction and discharge pipework, while a power meter provides the power absorbed by the motor. Motor efficiency from the motor manufacturer and drive losses are factored into the software, which then calculates pump operating efficiency. That reading is compared against the commissioning data to determine the drop in performance.

Advantages of thermodynamic monitoring
No dismantling of the pump is necessary.
Cost and energy savings by increasing pump availability and reliability.
Maintenance timing can be predicted and planned accurately, in line with predictive and planned strategies.
Where a flow meter is installed, the monitor can verify the accuracy of the meter by calculating Q from the empirical power relationship.

Capital cost is high, but return on investment may be realised quickly on large-capacity systems with numerous pumps. Alternatively the service can be purchased, or a provider contracted to measure and report periodically as part of the PPM programme.

Vibration monitoring

Online or offline depending on criticality. Online measurement uses proximity probes to measure shaft vibration. The most common form is offline using portable data collectors measuring casing vibration.

Vibration is measured for both the pump and its driver, at the bearing locations. Reporting requires a convention for labelling bearings, and the general convention is to start labelling from where the power comes in:

Sources of error in hand-held measurement

Hand-held measurements are subject to a number of errors, and personnel taking them must be aware of them. Errors arise from:

  • position on the machine;
  • probe angle;
  • probe type;
  • contact pressure.
Interpretation requires a baseline, not a limit

As established at commissioning, a base reading is taken at several operating points. Vibration levels are minimum at BEP and rise as the operating point moves away. Comparison against the original base readings is what indicates deterioration in mechanical health.

In the absence of a baseline, the limits in API 610 can be used as a reference — but a trend against the machine's own baseline is far more diagnostic than an absolute number against a standard.

Frequency analysis

Frequency analysis determines the nature of the fault. The three most common problems associated with pumps are easily detected:

  1. Unbalance
  2. Misalignment
  3. Bearing defects

Less frequent hydraulic problems can also be detected — cavitation, and improper impeller-to-volute-tongue gaps (which show at vane pass frequency).

Infrared thermography can be used alongside vibration analysis to pinpoint a problem source by detecting an elevated temperature at the trouble spot.

Oil and particle monitoring

Testing the oil and the particles in it is a good indicator of both bearing condition and the quality of the company's oil programme. Several tests determine bearing condition, or determine why a bearing failed so that appropriate corrective action follows:

  • spectrographic analysis;
  • viscosity analysis;
  • infrared analysis;
  • total acid number;
  • wear particle analysis and wear particle count;
  • moisture content.

Most of these must be performed under laboratory conditions, though portable instruments now allow some testing on site. Given that 0.002% water costs 48% of bearing life, the moisture content result alone justifies the programme.

System analysis

System analysis determines the system resistance curve of the installed pump, which can then be used to evaluate NPSH-a in the as-installed condition. A typical system analysis includes NPSH-a, NPSH-r, static head, friction loss through the system, and a complete review of the piping configuration, valves and fittings.

The pillar everyone skips, and the cost of skipping it

System analysis is often overlooked because it is assumed the system was constructed and the pumps are operated in accordance with the design specification. This is often not the case.

It is imperative to know where the pumps are being operated to perform a correct analysis — and that depends on the system. The process must also be understood, because it ultimately dictates how the pumps are operated.

All indicators may show the pump in distress when the real problem is that it is being run at low or high flow, with excessive hydraulic forces inside it. Overhaul that pump and it will come back in six months, because nothing about the cause was addressed.

What "failure" means

Pump maintenance is not primarily about dismantling and assembling pumps. It is an activity to prolong the efficient runtimes of the equipment and improve its overall effectiveness. The techniques above monitor health and, on indication of distress, indicate the cause and severity.

If a pump is in distress it should be taken up for corrective action immediately — prolonging maintenance lets primary failures cascade into secondary failures, increasing both downtime and cost. For non-essential pumps it may be acceptable to leave the machine unmonitored until failure.

The definition that changes how you manage pumps

"Failure" does not necessarily mean a seizure or a seal blowout forcing a shutdown. A pump is installed to perform a function under specified conditions. When it is no longer capable of performing that function under those conditions, it has failed.

So if a pump must deliver a certain flow at a certain horsepower, and horsepower consumption exceeds the specified limit, the pump has failed — even though it may be running perfectly smoothly. That reframing is what converts energy monitoring from a nice-to-have into a failure detection method.

Breakdown, diagnosis and removal

Typical failure causes in real applications:

  • mechanical seal failure;
  • excessive vibration;
  • pump rubbing or seizure;
  • inadequate performance — flow, head or power consumption;
  • leaking casing.
Diagnose before you dismantle

Before a pump is removed, establish the failure type. Once it is in pieces on the workshop floor, the evidence of why is largely gone.

Before removal

  1. Check with the operating crew on the perceived cause of failure.
  2. If it is safe to operate the pump, re-run it to diagnose the failure by:
    • sight, smell, noise and touch;
    • vibration analysis;
    • bearing temperature measurement;
    • a performance check.

These steps indicate the probable cause and — critically — establish whether it is specific to the pump or to the process or pumping system, including the suction and discharge systems.

Field checks if the problem is the pump

Pre-removal field check sequence

Single-stage pump strip-down

Once the pump is out for overhaul, the observation process does not end — it becomes more focused. Match and mark all parts before dismantling.

  1. Open the casing bolts and separate the casing or volute.
  2. Inspect the casing for corrosion and erosion. Inspect the gasket face particularly, to ensure sufficient land area and a smooth surface; repair appropriately if damaged.
  3. Inspect the impeller and nut for wear, corrosion and erosion. Inspect the vanes for pitting or cavities in the regions identified in Chapter 03 — this distinguishes cavitation from suction or discharge recirculation, and that distinction determines whether the fix is hydraulic or operational.
  4. Inspect the impeller and casing wear rings for signs of rub or detachment. A rub indicates excessive shaft deflection, improper assembly, or piping strain.
  5. Open the seal flange nuts and check the seal tension.
  6. Remove the impeller after opening the nut. Where it has balancing holes, unplug them. Inspect the backside wear rings carefully.
  7. Pull the shaft sleeve out together with the rotary head of the seal. Dismantle carefully and check for wear signs on the primary seal face and on the secondary seals — O-rings and gaskets.
  8. Check the bearings for roughness and measure the endplay.

Bearing removal and fits

Where bearings feel rough or there is excessive radial and axial float, dismantle the bearing housing to replace them. Normally the outer race has a transition fit with the housing and the inner races have an interference fit on the shaft, so the shaft with bearings can be drawn out of the housing with a slight tap. Bearings are removed from the shaft using a hydraulic press.

Once the bearings are off, measure the bore and shaft OD dimensions and work out the bearing fits. Check the shaft for straightness at this point — with the shaft bare, the V-block method is available — then polish and keep it ready for reassembly.

Repairing excessive bearing fits

Housings can be re-sleeved with rings of the same material. Shaft diameters can be subjected to weld build-up and re-machining — but this must consider the material of construction and the section thickness. Some materials need heat treatment, and a smaller-diameter shaft can warp if the weld procedure produces excessive heat build-up. Do not treat a weld repair on a small shaft as routine.

Preparation for reassembly

Impellers

The impeller is a rotating part subject to high velocity, and the adverse effects of poor hydraulic design show up as wear and erosion. Examine the vanes for cavitation and recirculation evidence — and communicate this information to operations, because the cause is in how the pump is being run.

Treat any corrosion of the impeller seriously and review the material of construction if corrosion rates are excessive. A badly worn or corroded impeller should be replaced. Where the impeller is to be reused, check the fit of its bore with the shaft, and its keyway.

Wear ring clearance — the highest-leverage measurement in the overhaul

Measure the wear ring diameter and compare with its mating ring in the casing. Clearances must be in line with the API 610 recommendations.

Where restoring clearance pays best

Wear ring clearance is probably the single factor with the greatest impact on pump efficiency — higher clearance means higher recirculation loss. And the percentage power loss from wear clearances is much higher in low specific speed pumps. Experimental data on double-suction pumps shows leakage losses as a percentage of power input falling steeply as specific speed rises.

So restoring clearances on low specific speed pumps gives the highest return in leakage loss reduction. Those pumps deserve the most attention on wear ring clearance. (After Karassik, When To Maintain Centrifugal Pumps, Hydrocarbon Processing, April 1993.)

Wear ring retention

Wear rings are press fitted to the impeller or casing and held by one of:

  1. tack welding, then grinding off the excess bead;
  2. threaded wear rings (threaded against the direction of rotation);
  3. drill, tap and fasten with grub screws radially — hole diameter no more than one-third of ring width;
  4. drill and tap at the ring/impeller or ring/casing interface and fasten with grub screws axially.

API 610 generally does not recommend the first two methods.

Balancing

After wear rings are renewed and machined to the requisite clearance, balance the impellers to the limits specified by API.

ISO 1940 G 6.3 is too liberal

The ISO 1940 standard recommendation of grade G 6.3 for centrifugal pumps is far too liberal. Use the API limits.

For pumps with long shafts, big impellers and heavier coupling halves, balance after assembling the complete rotor, and use two-plane rotor balancing in preference to single-plane balancing of the impeller alone.

Pump casing

Measure and record all fits of the casing and mating parts. Remove, clean, inspect and refit all plugs and fittings. Mounting pads must be flat and parallel to the pump centreline. Clean the casing and polish the gasket area with emery paper so no old gasket material adheres.

Internal coating — a genuine efficiency recovery

Where there are signs of corrosion or wear, consider coating the internals with a resistant epoxy or equivalent. The procedure:

  1. Descale the internals by ash, sand or grit blasting.
  2. For salt water applications, steam clean first to ensure all salt contamination is removed, then grit blast again.
  3. Apply the final epoxy coating.
Why this is worth doing

Such coatings smooth the surface roughness of the liquid path and so reduce hydraulic losses inside the pump. The reported impact on efficiency can be as high as 10–15% on a badly pitted casing. Few overhaul activities return that much.

Clean the casing bolts, arrange the correct number, and coat with anti-seize compound.

Seal and bearing housings

Cleanliness of the seal and bearing housings is of utmost importance — it has a large impact on the life of the bearings and seals.

  • Where the seal housing has cooling water jackets, get them chemically cleaned periodically.
  • Inspect and check the bearing housing mating faces with the seal housing for proper fit.
  • Check the bearing housing cover faces for smooth surface finish. Replace the oil seals or any other shaft seal.
  • Check the concentricity of the bearing housing bores — place the housing on a horizontal boring machine with the dial gauge on the boring bar and measure in the vertical and horizontal planes. Alternatively use a dummy shaft with zero clearance in the housing bore.
  • Paint the internals of the housing with oil-resistant paint. Bearing housings are typically cast iron, and in high-humidity, high-rainfall areas the inner surface corrodes. Corrosion dust washed around by the splashing oil acts as an abrasive for the bearings. Inspect the paint condition before assembly.
  • Seal the housing against moisture ingress — install the bearing covers with proper bearing isolators.
  • Clean the oil level gauge opening and fit a new gauge. Remove, clean and refit the bearing housing vent.

Shaft

Check for straightness, thread condition at the impeller nut location, keyway fit at impeller and coupling locations, and fits for the anti-friction bearings. Dress up or repair rounded shoulders and steps. Inspect the lip seal area; if damaged, repair it or compensate with a sleeve of appropriate size.

Mechanical seals

Mechanical seals are the prime cause of pump failure. Efforts to improve seal reliability led to the standard API 682, which recommends the use of cartridge seals. Cartridge seals eliminate the skilled workmanship required to assemble a seal at the user's end — which is exactly where seal assembly errors occur.

Pump shaft sleeve

Seal assembly preparation begins with the sleeve condition. The sleeve area under secondary seals such as O-rings or PTFE wedges is most prone to fretting. To eliminate the problem, that area can be plasma sprayed with a hard ceramic coating — chromium oxide, tungsten carbide or aluminium oxide — giving nearly 70 HRc, then ground to the exact dimension.

Seal faces and flatness

Seal faces should be renewed. Check the faces for flatness using an interferometer and optical flat: a monochromatic light source (helium or sodium) illuminates the piece through an optical flat placed on it, and the light reflects back through the flat producing interference light bands.

That is a helium light band, measuring 0.3 microns (0.0000116 in). Concentric rings indicate the surface is neither concave nor convex; when it is, the concentric rings give way to arcs. The number of arcs or rings cut by a straight line across the face is the number of light bands.

Allowable flatness
3 light bands (0.9 µm)

That degree of flatness allows a mechanical seal to seal vacuum down to one Torr (1 mm Hg), and lets the seal comfortably pass fugitive emission specifications of less than 100 ppm.

Do not relap carbon/graphite faces

Carbon graphite faces relax after lapping. Although lapped to less than one light band by the seal manufacturer, you may read as high as three light bands on check. These faces return to flat once placed against a hard face that is flat — so the reading is not necessarily a defect.

Carbon/graphite faces should not be relapped, because the relapping procedure drives the trapped solids further into the face. Do not use lapping powder or paste on carbon/graphite. Lap them dry on ceramic stones of varying grit or finish.

Rotary head and springs

The rotary head housing one of the seal faces comprises a stainless steel retainer which may house a single or multiple springs, and may have anti-rotation provision. Check all of it is capable of performing its function. It is advisable to replace the springs. Unscrew, clean and reuse the grub screws fixing the retainer to the sleeve. Where bellows seals are used, inspect carefully for cracks at the weld joints.

Secondary seals

Once the faces are checked, wrap and store them carefully in an air-conditioned, dust-free room until reassembly. Secondary seals include all O-rings and gaskets used with the stationary seat, rotating head and sleeve. During inspection, if any O-ring is found swelled, shrunk, corroded, nicked, cut, extruded or blistered, investigate the cause.

Never reuse a secondary seal

For no reason should the secondary seals be reused. The savings will not justify even a pump leaking on the test bench — let alone in service. Procure a new set with confirmed dimensions and keep it with the seal faces.

Seal plate and seal housing

The seal plate housing the stationary seat must be free of corrosion. Where fitted with a stop pin to prevent seat rotation, check the pin's condition and replace if necessary. Clean and polish the gasket/O-ring surface.

The seal housing must be clean and dry; internals may be coated with an anti-rust compound. The throat bush is fitted to the seal housing — confirm its clearance with the sleeve OD and replace if higher. Where it has integral cooling water jackets, flush and periodically chemically clean them.

Bearings and coupling

Leave new bearings packed until required for assembly. Confirm the housing fits with the bearing where there are repeated bearing failures or high bearing temperatures.

Inspect the coupling for wear and its fit with the shaft; if normal, grease where applicable.

The clearance / overhaul chart

Preparation for assembly is a detailed exercise that lays the basis for a quick assembly and a reliable repair. It requires acceptable clearances and accurate data so that assembly is not hampered by incorrect dimensions or unclear expectations of a sub-assembly procedure. The consolidated chart is on the formula and data sheet.

Single-stage pump assembly

Bearing assembly

  1. Apply oil lightly on the bearing seat of the shaft.
  2. Shielding, if any, must face the proper direction. Angular contact bearings must also face the proper direction, and duplex bearings must be mounted with the proper faces together — arrangements vary from model to model, so refer to the OEM manual.
  3. Press the bearing on squarely — do not cock it onto the shaft. The sleeve used to press it on must be clean, cut square, and contact the inner race only.
  4. Press the bearing firmly against the shaft shoulder; the shoulder helps support and square the bearing.
  5. Where fits are tight, heat the bearings with an induction heater with a de-magnetising cycle. Temperature must not exceed 110 °C.
  6. Install the snap rings with the flat side against the bearing, and tighten the lock nuts.
  7. Lubricate the bearings.
  8. Wrap the assembly in plastic while the bearing housing is made ready.
  9. Before placing the rotor in the housing, ensure the housing is spotlessly clean.
  10. In pumps where the inboard bearing (closer to the impeller) is the locating (fixed) bearing, fix the bearing cover that provides the step to the outer race — after applying sealing compound to the mating faces.
  11. Smear oil on the bearing housing bores.
  12. Tap the shaft with mounted bearings into the housing until the outer race of the inboard bearing rests against the step.
  13. Apply sealing compound to the outboard mating face for the bearing cover and tighten the bolts.
The alternative arrangement worth knowing

In another arrangement the outboard bearing is the locating bearing, placed in a housing that can be screw-jacked axially after loosening the clamping bolts. The advantage: the rotor can be moved axially without dismantling the pump, which is particularly useful for setting the clearance between a back-vaned impeller and the casing wall.

Assembly: bearings fitted on the shaft, lock nut tightened against the outboard bearing, housing slid over the outer race, snap ring slipped into the slot, then the rotor assembly placed in the bearing housing and the bolts tightened.

Seal assembly and seal tension

  1. Clamp the stationary seat firmly with the seals in the seal end plate.
  2. Bolt that to the seal housing.
  3. Slide the seal housing carefully over the shaft so there is no impact between the shaft surface and the stationary seat.
  4. Bolt it to the bearing housing, observing the match marks made before dismantling. This is necessary because if even a single bolthole is out of phase, it may be impossible to make the sealant connections in the field.
  5. Adjust the seal tension.

The springs have a certain effect on face pressure, so a certain seal tension must be maintained. For multi-spring designs, seal tension is typically of the order of 3–4 mm depending on seal size; single-spring seals have a much higher seal tension.

Seal tension = F − (S + A) = 3 mm
with a gasket: Seal tension = F − (S + A + G)

F
free length of the assembled rotary head — from the edge of the sleeve to the seal face, without compression
A
distance between the shaft step and the face of the stationary seat (measured directly, or using a frame of reference based on the pump construction)
S
length of the sleeve — the difference between the easily measurable lengths L and M
G
gasket thickness, where the sleeve design uses a gasket rather than an O-ring to seal the shaft OD / sleeve ID gap

Where seal tension is improper, cuts can be taken on the sleeve steps. Caution: ensure the step of the balance carbon and the sleeve step do not foul up once the seal is under compression.

  1. Squirt oil from a can to lubricate the seal faces.
  2. Insert the sleeve with the rotary head.
Cartridge seals remove this whole step

The seal tension adjustment above can be eliminated entirely by using cartridge seals, which come with pre-adjusted seal tension — tension is achieved simply by releasing the setting tabs. This is why API 682 pushes cartridge construction, and why it is worth specifying on any pump that will be overhauled in a site workshop.

Impeller and casing assembly

  1. With the sleeve and rotary head in place, compression is achieved by installing the impeller and locking it to the shaft with the nut.
  2. After the impeller is fixed, measure the runout of the impeller wear ring in the installed condition — within 0.05 mm.
  3. Place the casing gasket in the pump casing.
  4. Bolt the casing to the seal housing flange, again observing the match marks.
  5. Tighten the bolts to the specified torque.
Torque and anti-seize — the correction people forget

Correct bolting torque depends on bolt size, thread and material — and critically on whether the bolt has been lubricated. If an anti-seize compound has been applied, the tightening torque must be set to approximately two-thirds of the torque specified for dry bolts.

Apply anti-seize and then torque to the dry figure, and you have over-stressed every casing bolt.

Seal hydrotest

Where the workshop can test seals, do it.

  1. Bolt blind flanges to the inlet and outlet nozzles.
  2. The blind flanges should be drilled and tapped for fittings to fill the pump with water and vent air from the topmost point.
  3. Fill with water and vent all air out.
  4. Pressurise to a minimum of 1–2 kg/cm² above the shut-off discharge pressure.
  5. Once pressurised, use compressed air to dry the pump externally, especially near the seal and shaft interface — otherwise you cannot tell a leak from residual water.
  6. Rotate the shaft slowly by hand and observe carefully for leaks from the seal.
  7. Examine the casing joint too.
  8. Investigate any drop in hydrotest pressure.
  9. If satisfactory, release the pressure and drain the water.

Coupling, lines and fittings

  • Mount the pump coupling half on the shaft. The shaft end should preferably be flush with the coupling half — but if it was not originally, that should have been recorded and kept to the same arrangement.
  • Fit the sealant lines, oil level gauges, cooling water lines and any others.
  • After removing the blind flanges, cover the pump nozzles completely with tape.

The pump is ready for installation at site.

Vertical pump repair

A vertical pump comprises:

  • Head / driver assembly — an electric motor and a cast or fabricated base from which the column and bowl assembly is suspended;
  • Column and shaft assembly — the column pipe links head assembly to bowl assembly and conducts the liquid from bowl to discharge. Inside it runs the pump shaft transmitting power from driver to impellers. The pumped liquid generally lubricates the line shaft bearings in the column.
  • Pump bowl assembly — each bowl comprises impeller, suction and discharge casing.

There are three reasons to overhaul a vertical turbine pump:

  1. Complete breakdown leading to unexpected disruption of a critical system.
  2. Reduced efficiency from increased clearances between bowls and enclosed impeller skirts, increased clearance between bowl shaft and bearings, and breakdown of the fluid passages. Over time, running a badly worn pump can be just as costly as some system shutdowns.
  3. Changed operational requirements in pressure and capacity.
Why vertical pumps are harder

A vertical pump has far more components than a horizontal pump, and its maintenance problems are different and substantially greater. The rotor is not stabilised by gravity and is prone to gyroscopic effects that can damage the rotor and the casing. Repair demands corresponding care.

Dismantling

The procedure is influenced by construction, and repair is much more complex than a single-stage pump. It is advisable to prepare a datasheet for the pump comprising all pump details, clearance and fits data, and any special remarks and procedures.

  1. Lay the pump horizontally on the floor with the discharge nozzle facing the ground.
  2. Take a paintbrush and match mark all the joints. Because bowl joints look similar, punch consecutive numbers on the joints — taking care these are not in addition to numbers from a previous overhaul, which would cause considerable confusion during assembly.
  3. Push the pump upper shaft toward the suction and measure the distance to the motor adapter face, and the adapter face configuration.
  4. Pull the rotor outward and re-measure that distance to get the total axial float of the rotor.
  5. Open the bowls. They may be individually screwed together, bolted with a series of bolts around the bowl flange (the most common design), or bolted with tie bolts extending from the bottom of the pumping section to the top through the bowls. Open one section at a time.
  6. Where a split collet bushing locks the impeller on the shaft, measure and record the distance between the shaft end and the impeller.
  7. Measure and record the float at each stage.
  8. As disassembly progresses, match mark the impellers, collets, keys, locking pins, split keeps and keeper plates, and arrange them in an organised manner.
  9. Match mark the line shaft couplings along the mating shaft at each end.
Removing line shaft couplings without destroying the shaft

Remove line shaft couplings using pipe or chain tongs on the mating shafts. Wrenches applied to the couplings can cause the coupling to collapse and seize on the shaft. If normal methods fail, cut the coupling with a thin grinding wheel. The main aim is to prevent damage to the shaft, as it is the most expensive item.

Check the rigid coupling halves from the motor and pump for trueness and squareness.

Preparation for assembly

Bowl and impeller fluid passages

Examine carefully for wear. A significant impact on efficiency results if they are damaged, pitted, have particle build-up, or have eroded coatings. Coatings are normally applied to the interior of the bowl assembly, mainly to enhance efficiency or protect internals — but fluid velocity and suspended solids often damage the coating. Cleaning, recoating or replacing the affected parts are options depending on severity.

Shaft

The bearing areas are susceptible to wear. If condition is good, measure the shaft runout: shaft on V-blocks or knife-edge rollers at the recommended distances, dial gauge at various locations, rotated a full circle by hand.

Wear rings and impellers

Measure and record impeller-to-bowl wear ring clearances; they should be in accordance with API 610. Where the bowl has no wear ring, check whether sufficient material is available to bore the bowl and/or turn the impellers. Wear rings can replace the worn material and bring parts back into tolerance.

Where the fluid contains particularly abrasive solids, the wear rings are made of materials chosen to minimise abrasive erosion. If wear rings are ordered with the original unit for easy replacement later, both bowl and impeller pieces are necessary to return the bowl assembly to its original tolerances.

Balance all impellers individually to API specifications. Check all bearing bores for parallelism with the shaft column.

Line shaft bushings

Measure all bushings in the bearing retainers for clearance with the shaft. If replaced for excessive clearance, secure them properly to the retainer per their design and material. These bushings generally have a spiral groove for lubrication, as clearances are on the tighter side.

Column pipe and tubes

The pipe may be pitted, encrusted with scale, or have damaged coating. Considering the degree of roughness, decide whether to reuse, recoat, clean or replace. Inspect the tube faces for leakage or damage and replace the tubes where necessary. Enclosing tube bearings should have approximately the same tolerances as the bowl bearings (10 mils / 0.25 mm).

An alternative drive shaft configuration uses a separate oil-lubricated column, offering extended bearing performance at higher first cost, with payback through the service life.

Open line shaft bearings should be replaced when visibly damaged. They are usually rubber, and their condition does not necessarily affect efficiency — but it is vital for supporting the shaft.

Discharge head and seals

The discharge head generally requires the least maintenance. Most repairs are on the shaft seal, whether a stuffing box or a mechanical seal. Replace packing in the stuffing box to control leakage. Check stuffing box or seal-housing bearings to make sure the shaft is properly supported. Mechanical seal repair is not very different from the horizontal case.

The strongest case for cartridge seals

In vertical pumps cartridge seals make even more sense than in horizontal ones. On a horizontal overhung-impeller pump, the rotor must be dismantled to reach the seal even if it is a cartridge type. But on in-between-bearing pumps and vertical pumps the impeller assembly need not be touched, so seal replacement can be done on site without removing the pump to a shop.

And because changing seals on site is tricky and error-prone given site conditions, that makes a strong case for cartridge seals in these pump types.

Driver and thrust bearing

When a vertical pump is started it has a momentary up-thrust hydraulic action. Operated at high flows continuously, it is likely to have a continuous upward axial force. This stresses the motor thrust bearing and creates the probability of buckled line shafts, rubbing impellers and leaking mechanical seals. Check the thrust bearings to ensure they are not excessively worn.

Motors upgraded to premium efficiency, or at least replaced or rewound, can significantly increase overall pump performance.

Vertical pump assembly

  • Assemble the pump in the vertical position. This prevents the risk of shaft or bowl assembly developing sag. Bowl bushing clearance is typically 9 mils and bowl spigot fits are 2–3 mils, so in a horizontal position it is bound to touch after 3–4 bowl assemblies. In the vertical position those clearances are distributed.
  • Use the jack-bolt in the suction piece to position the end of the shaft and allow accurate spacing of the impellers. A load applied on previously installed impellers can knock them off their collet while installing the next one — and if impellers become loose, there is a grave danger of a rub on starting.
  • Impellers fitted with collets need much more attention than those held by split rings, keys or snap rings. Excessive tightening of the collet can crack the impeller in the hub area.
  • After the bowls are assembled, check the lift and rotate the rotor by hand to check for any internal rubbing.
  • Where the pump is long, column sections have to be assembled horizontally. Rotate the assembled position 180° with the installation of every additional component — this staggers the alignment clearances and fits through the column length and keeps the assembly along the shaft centreline.
  • After the columns are fitted, install the discharge head. At this stage, compare the shaft extension length against the figure taken before dismantling. Deviations of 1/16 to 1/8 in are normal and can be compensated with gaskets.
  • The shaft extension may be supported and the shaft locked before dispatch to site. If the jack bolt at the suction bell is left in place, it must carry a warning tag to remove it before installation.

Multistage (barrel / double case) pump repair

A barrel-type double case pump has two casings. The inner assembly comprises the complex shape of the stationary hydraulic passages; it sees the external differential pressure, so any bolting to hold it is minimal. It may be split axially into similar halves cast from the same pattern, or be of the multiple diffuser casing type. The outer casing acts as the pressure boundary for the pumped liquid and is designed as a pressure vessel.

The rotor is either opposed impeller or in-line; the latter has a balance disk to handle the much higher axial thrust. These pumps serve as boiler feed water pumps, charge pumps on high-temperature high-pressure hydrocarbons, water-flood pumps and pipeline pumps — all critical, all with expensive downtime.

The spare inner assembly strategy

Some designs have a fully separable inner case subassembly including the rotor, removable by taking off the outboard cover without breaking the suction line joint.

Because downtime is expensive, a spare inner subassembly is usually made ready and held as a spare — during a repair the inner assemblies are simply swapped. For designs that include the journal and thrust bearing in the subassembly, downtime is reduced further because even those adjustments are made beforehand.

Shaft checks and balancing

Shaft runout
< 1 mil
At bearing areas
< 0.5 mil

In some designs impellers are shrink fitted on the shaft to prevent mechanical looseness, positioned axially by split rings on the suction side of each impeller hub. The grooves for the split rings cause the shaft to develop runout, and because of the shrink fits the shaft fits must be measured and recorded.

Next, balance the shaft with the half keys taped: mount on a balancing machine, spin to 300 rpm for about 10 minutes, measure shaft runout at mid-span and mark the angular position. Achieve balance by machining the face of the steps of the shaft, then re-check runout.

Stacking the rotor for balancing

  1. Place the shaft vertically.
  2. Stack all rotating components sequentially — impellers, thrust collar, balance drum if installed.
  3. Since most components are shrink fit, heat them to about 110–120 °C depending on bore and shaft diameters.
  4. Hold the match-marked split ring and key for the first-stage impeller in place. Mark the impeller keyway with a pen if it is blind at one end.
  5. Heat the impeller to 120 °C and slip it carefully over the key to position until it contacts the split rings.
  6. Apply constant downward pressure until the impeller has firmly gripped the shaft. To aid this, pass cooling air from the impeller OD to the suction eye until the impeller is at ambient.
  7. Install the other impellers the same way.
  8. Once all impellers are firmly fixed, measure rotor runout. If within limits, tighten the impeller nuts and re-check runout.
  9. If acceptable, fit all rotating components and send for balancing. If excessive runout is detected, the shaft nuts are the probable cause and their faces should be rectified.

For the balancing procedure itself, see W. Edward Nelson, Monitoring Repairs to Your Pumps, 12th International Pump Users Symposium, 1995.

Final stacking with diffusers

Once balanced, unstack the rotor. Place the shaft vertically again and this time stack the impellers together with the diffuser elements, in the same manner.

  • After installation of every stage, check the float. A jack may be placed under the coupling end of the shaft; the required lift must be maintained throughout the assembly.
  • Diffuser covers have a small interference fit of 0.01 mm and may require heat. If a cover does not fit, heat the preceding cover with a small torch around the entire circumference for a minute, then tap the cover lightly with a hammer.
  • After all impellers and diffusers are installed, install the balance piston. Once cool, tighten the nut to the match marks made earlier.
  • The impeller–diffuser line-up — the axial location of the rotor to give the correct gap — is adjusted by the thickness of the locator ring placed between the thrust collar and the shaft step.

Impeller to diffuser axial overlap

The relation of shroud and hub positions to the diffuser/volute and casing must be verified and determined during assembly. Axially, the impeller discharge should be entirely within the axial width of the diffuser. If it is not, flow from the impeller discharges against the diffuser or casing walls.

Rotor sensitivity to impeller and diffuser alignment depends on the ratio B₂/B₃ — impeller exit vane width to diffuser vane width — and must be judged case by case. A typical overlap of not less than half the impeller side-plate (shroud/cover) thickness is preferred.

A balancing operation that destroys performance

During dynamic balancing, if grinding of the impeller sidewall extends to the impeller OD such that it affects the overlap, pump performance will be affected. The balancing shop needs to know this constraint; it will not infer it.

Gap-A and Gap-B

Beyond axial location, two famous dimensions must be measured, recorded and corrected during overhaul. They have a pronounced impact on the hydraulic stability of any high-energy pump.

Where the number of impeller and diffuser vanes is even, the radial gap must be larger by 4%.

The one correction that costs nothing and gains everything

Correct gaps eliminate vane pass vibration frequency. And critically: machining the diffuser or volute vane to achieve the right Gap-B without affecting Gap-A causes no loss of efficiency. In fact, the high noise level, shock and vibration caused by vane pass frequency are eliminated — improving efficiency.

This is why the data sheet should demand the impeller-to-cutwater clearance for pumps developing more than 200 mlc.

Inserting the inner assembly and final build

  1. Insert the assembled inner subassembly — inner casing plus rotor — into the outer casing using a guide fixture.
  2. Install the expansion gaskets and spacers between inner casing and outer head. Improper inner head gasket spacers can result in improper makeup of the intermediate diffuser covers; the suction spacer and discharge diffuser spacer could then allow liquid recirculation, dropping efficiency and causing erosion. Spacer thickness plus gasket should achieve approximately 0.4–0.5 mm compression on each gasket.
  3. Install and tighten the head. In most designs the head fit is all-metal and the gap at the head should be approximately 1–1.5 mm.
  4. Install the bearing housings with the lower half journal bearing in place. Move the coupling end housing until the suction impeller is centred in the wear rings within 0.025 mm — measurable with feeler gauges.
  5. Record the dial gauge readings of the seal housing bores and faces.
  6. Insert the locating spacer and the thrust collar.
  7. Install the seals before the bearings are placed in the housings.
  8. Insert the bearing housing dowels and bolt them.
  9. Finally install the journal and thrust bearings. The location of the thrust collar lines up the rotor axially to get the right gap between impeller and diffuser.
  10. Install the coupling halves and make ready for alignment.

When to overhaul — the economics

Pump performance deteriorates over time, and it is always a debate whether to live with the problem or overhaul. Living with it means continuous loss of money through inefficient operation; overhauling means incurring the maintenance cost. The question is answerable arithmetically.

The algorithm below is the work of Ray Beebe, from Condition Monitoring of Pumps can save Money (APMA Seminar).

The two deterioration assumptions

Which assumption applies, and what follows
AssumptionMethod
Deterioration is constant over time A cash flow analysis establishes whether the investment in overhaul gives the required rate of return — the same process used for any plant improvement investment.
Deterioration rate increases with time The optimum time for overhaul is when the accumulated cost of the increased power consumption equals the cost of the overhaul.

Three operational contexts

  1. Deterioration limits plant throughput. The loss of production is high enough to justify the overhaul cost immediately — it becomes a matter of finding an opportune window.
  2. Pump operation is intermittent and does not affect throughput. Any deterioration simply means longer operating duration — typically pumps loading product into tankers. The additional operating time and excess power can be compared directly with the overhaul cost.
  3. Deterioration is initially offset by opening a control valve or increasing speed, but over time a threshold is reached and throughput begins to suffer. In some cases the power requirement exceeds the motor rating, causing overload trips.

Use the performance calculation procedure to determine the power the pump consumes, benchmark it against the performance curves, and the difference gives the excess power consumption — which converts directly into lost expense. Pumps consuming excess power are the primary candidates and take higher priority.

Worked example

Context 2 — the pump runs intermittently and loss of performance does not affect throughput.

Caveats and extensions

Honest limits of the method
  • The calculation is only correct if wear progresses at a uniformly increasing rate with time. Information may not be available to make any other assumption, but decision makers have to start somewhere.
  • Some relatively small pumps may never justify overhaul on energy savings alone — but may be justified on reduced plant production rate, where the deterioration does not affect production at least initially.
Variable speed pumps deteriorate much more expensively

For a pump whose speed is varied to meet its duty, the effect of wear on power required is much more dramatic than for a constant-speed throttle-controlled pump — because power increases in proportion to the speed ratio cubed.

Unless the pump output is limited by reaching maximum speed or the driver's highest allowable power output, no production will be lost — so the deterioration is invisible in production terms. But power consumed increases far more dramatically for a given wear state than on a constant-speed pump. This is exactly where performance monitoring tools or portable instruments earn their cost, because nothing else will tell you.

The same method and calculations then find the overhaul time for minimum total cost. The algorithm is general: wherever there is deterioration in performance leading to loss of energy, it is a handy tool for maintenance engineers and managers managing assets to provide capacity for production.
