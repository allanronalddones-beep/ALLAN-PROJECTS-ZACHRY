Chapter 02 · Core reference

Design and Construction

The parts that decide how a pump performs and how long it lasts: impellers, casings and diffusers, wear rings, shafts and sleeves, packing, mechanical seals, bearing housings and couplings.

The basic components of a centrifugal pump are the same in nearly every design. What changes — and what decides whether a machine is fit for its duty — is how each component is constructed. This chapter works outward from the impeller.

Impellers

The impeller converts shaft rotation into liquid velocity. Liquid enters the eye, is guided from inlet to outlet by vanes, and leaves at the periphery. The vanes are normally cast integrally with a back plate (the back shroud or back cover) and, in closed designs, a front plate.

Impellers are almost always castings; fabricated and welded impellers are rare. The casting process is where impeller quality is won. Liquid-contact surfaces must be as smooth as possible, so the core sand mixture and the core finish matter directly — the relative smoothness of the liquid path is one of the largest determinants of pump efficiency. Disk friction losses scale with roughly the fifth power of diameter, so a large closed impeller with a rough surface is penalised severely.

After casting and surface finishing, impellers are dynamically balanced. ISO 1940 specifies residual unbalance limits; API imposes stricter ones. Balancing the impeller alone is not sufficient — once the rotor components are ready they should be mounted assembly-wise on the balancing machine and balanced as an assembly.

Open impeller sub-types

  • Fully scalloped — the back shroud is almost entirely removed, so hydraulic axial thrust is nearly eliminated.
  • Partially scalloped — greater axial thrust than fully scalloped, but better head and efficiency.
  • Fully back-shrouded open — around 5% higher efficiency than fully scalloped, but reduced head generation and the highest axial thrust of the open types. Back vanes are normally added to relieve the pressure on the back shroud.

Most open impellers in service are partially scalloped or fully back-shrouded. Fully open impellers are rare because of poor efficiency and the bending load carried by unsupported vanes.

Non-clog and vortex impellers

Where solids are large, or are crystals or fibres, a vortex (non-clog) impeller is used — a fully shrouded open type that does not impart energy to the liquid directly. Instead it creates a vortex, and the vortex drives the pumpage. The impeller sits above the volute and therefore experiences almost no radial force, which allows extended running even at closed discharge. Closed and semi-open non-clog variants (two- and three-passage closed, S-shaped two-passage semi-open) also exist.

Single and double suction

A single-suction impeller has one eye. It is simple, but flow entering from one side only leaves the impeller axially unbalanced.

A double-suction impeller has suction cavities on both sides. It is hydraulically balanced and has a lower NPSH-r than an equivalent single-suction impeller — but it is vulnerable to uneven flow split if the suction piping is poor. Flows above roughly 550 m³/h (153 l/s) commonly call for double suction.

Note on specific speed

When calculating specific speed, use total BEP flow with no correction for double suction. When calculating suction specific speed, halve the flow for a double-suction impeller. This asymmetry trips people up regularly.

Flow outlet direction and specific speed

The flow direction leaving the impeller — radial, mixed or axial — is set by specific speed, and the geometry changes progressively with it. Low specific speed (low flow, high head) calls for radial impellers; high specific speed (high flow, low head) calls for axial propellers. The transition is conventionally tracked by the ratio of outlet to inlet diameter:

Casings and energy recovery

Liquid leaves the impeller outlet at 30–40 m/s. It must reach the discharge pipe at 3–7 m/s. That velocity reduction — and the conversion of kinetic energy into pressure — happens in the casing, in a device generically called a recuperator. Doing it with minimal loss is the whole design problem.

The recuperator options are: vaneless guide ring, concentric casing, volute casing, vaned diffuser ring, diagonal diffuser vanes and axial diffuser vanes.

Vaneless guide ring

Two smooth discs with constant spacing, or spacing increasing toward the outlet. Pressure recovery is proportional to the ratio of outlet to inlet diameter (Do/Di); ring breadth plays little part in head generation, though constant-breadth rings prove more efficient than those widening at outlet. Because high head would demand an impractically large outlet diameter, vaneless guide rings are confined to low-head pumps — typically mixed-flow impellers at higher specific speed with a constant-section annular delivery passage, and occasionally low specific speed pumps handling solids.

Concentric casing

Found in single-stage pumps and in the last stage of multistage pumps. Impeller diameter to casing diameter ratio is held between 1.15 and 1.2. Volute width accommodates the maximum impeller width; capacity at best efficiency is controlled by the volute diameter. A cutwater tongue minimises recirculation in the volute and significantly reduces radial load on the shaft.

Below Ns 600 (US units) a concentric casing is more efficient than a conventional volute; above 600 its efficiency falls away progressively. Choose a concentric casing when:

  • flow is low and head high, with Ns in the 500–600 range;
  • one casing must accommodate several impeller sizes;
  • the casing is fabricated rather than cast;
  • the volute passage must be machined from a casting;
  • foundry limitations force a wider impeller.
Counter-intuitive behaviour

In a concentric casing, uniform velocity and pressure distribution occurs at zero flow, and uniformity is progressively lost as flow rises — so radial load at BEP is considerably higher than at no-flow. This is the exact opposite of a volute casing. See Chapter 04.

Volute casing

The most common design. With smooth surfaces, hydraulic losses are negligible, and impeller vanes and shrouds can be trimmed with minimal efficiency penalty. Kinetic energy converts to pressure only in the diffusion chamber immediately past the volute throat, where the divergence angle is between 7° and 13°.

Volute cross-sections are circular, trapezoidal (typical of single-stage pumps) or rectangular. Rectangular sections are used in small single-stage and in multistage pumps; they are economical because pattern cost and production time are low, and hydraulic losses are minimal below Ns 1100.

Single versus double volute

A single volute designed on constant velocity is the most efficient of all casing types, easy to cast and least expensive. Its weakness is radial load: pressure distribution is balanced only at BEP. At low flow, impeller surfaces nearest the discharge see high pressure while those beyond the cutwater see comparatively low pressure. The resulting unbalanced force can be taken as acting at a point 240° from the cutwater, directed toward the impeller centre, and is maximum at shut-off and almost zero at BEP.

A double volute is two single volutes in opposed arrangement, with total throat area equal to the comparable single volute. It limits radial force substantially. The volute is symmetrical about its centreline, but the two passages carrying liquid to the discharge flange are not, so pressure forces around the impeller periphery do not cancel completely and some residual radial force remains.

Because double volutes hold up better across the operating range, they are preferred wherever flow varies — which is most process duty above the casting size threshold.

Vaned diffuser ring

A ring of symmetrically placed vanes around the impeller forming gradually widening passages; flow from the diffuser is collected in a volute or circular casing. The narrowest section of each passage is the throat. Design parallels the volute, except that a diffuser has many throats where a volute has one continuous expanding section. Past the throat the channel area increases progressively, giving a further slight pressure rise. A straight diffusing channel is marginally more efficient but produces a larger casing.

Vane count convention: the number of diffuser vanes is usually one more than the number of impeller vanes. Diffuser vanes should not greatly outnumber impeller vanes, and the one-more rule ensures a single impeller passage does not span several diffuser passages. Velocity distribution around the impeller is more uniform than in a volute, so radial loads are much lower.

Diagonal and axial diffuser vanes

Diagonal diffuser vanes serve mixed-flow impellers. They turn the liquid leaving the impeller to run along the pump axis, and reduce velocity to recover pressure. Vanes are disposed axially with no sudden area changes, which allows one diffuser model to serve impellers of different diameters and breadths. As specific speed rises, impeller and diffuser profiles approach propeller-pump shapes.

Axial diffuser vanes sit behind the impeller of an axial flow pump, serving the same two functions. They usually number 5–8, with the lower count on lower specific speed pumps. Efficiency is influenced by passage shape — vane count, axial length and the gap between impeller blades and diffuser vanes. Shorter vanes in greater number give better efficiency for the same duty. At high enough specific speed the vanes become superfluous and a simple conical diffuser replaces them.

Wear rings

The impeller rotates inside a stationary casing, so a running clearance is unavoidable — and because there is a pressure difference across it, pumped liquid recirculates through it. That leakage costs efficiency.

Tightening the clearance improves efficiency, reduces erosion from suction recirculation, and improves rotor-dynamic stability (so the pump runs with lower vibration). But it increases the risk of impeller-to-casing contact, which can scrap two expensive parts. The answer is to fit sacrificial rings: an impeller wear ring on the impeller eye and a case wear ring in the casing. Wear is then confined to cheap, replaceable components.

Rings at the impeller eye are front wear rings. Rings are also fitted at the back shroud, typically when the impeller carries balancing holes to reduce axial thrust — see Chapter 04.

Wear ring materials

The governing requirement is resistance to seizure on contact. Materials with galling tendencies — SS 316 notably — are therefore not used here. Favoured materials include:

  • Austenitic grey iron castings — ASTM A436 Type 1
  • Austenitic ductile iron castings — ASTM A439 Type D2
  • 12% chrome steel — AISI 420 (hardenable)
  • 18Cr–8Ni steel castings — AISI 304
  • Copper alloy sand castings (bronze) — ASTM B584 Alloy C90500
  • Aluminium bronze sand castings — ASTM B148 Alloy C95800
  • Monel K500, Nickel 200

Hardness is deliberately mismatched so wear concentrates on the stationary part: case ring at roughly 225–275 BHN, impeller ring 50–100 BHN harder, in the 325–375 BHN range.

Thermoplastic composite rings

Thermoplastic composites — often carbon-fibre reinforced — are increasingly used, either on the stationary part alone or with a steel mating component. They are non-galling, have a lower coefficient of friction, and show excellent wear resistance in clean liquids. The greater hardness differential lets the thermoplastic act as the sacrificial element, and the reduced galling tendency allows roughly half the clearance that metal rings require — a direct efficiency gain, particularly on low specific speed pumps.

Their limitations: maximum life is obtained only in clean fluids, and chemical compatibility is narrower than metals.

Ring retention

API 610 does not recommend tack welding rings to impellers. Rings should be pressed in and secured with locking pins or threaded dowels, radially or axially. In practice the retention methods seen are tack welding with the bead ground off, threaded rings (threaded against the direction of rotation), radial grub screws (hole diameter no more than one-third of ring width), and axial grub screws at the ring/impeller interface. API generally disallows the first two.

Shaft and shaft sleeves

The rotor assembly comprises shaft, impeller, sleeves, the rotating seal element, bearings or bearing surfaces, and coupling half. The shaft is its key element and is a genuinely stressed member: during operation it sees tension, compression, bending and torsion, all cyclic — so fatigue is the expected failure mode.

Shaft design is based either on torsional shear stress at the smallest diameter or, properly, on a comprehensive fatigue evaluation combining the loads, cycle count and stress concentration factors — invariably by finite element analysis. Stress is not the only criterion; the design must also satisfy:

  • shaft deflection (see Chapter 04);
  • key stresses;
  • mounted component fits;
  • critical speeds — rotordynamics.

Shaft sleeves

Mechanical seals and gland packing in contact with the shaft cause wear by friction and fretting corrosion. A shaft sleeve is fitted as a sacrificial component so the seal or packing runs against the sleeve, not the shaft — it is far cheaper to replace a sleeve than a complete shaft.

Sleeve material generally mirrors the shaft, though standardisation favours SS 316. The portion exposed to the secondary seal (an O-ring or PTFE wedge) is hard coated: plasma-sprayed chrome oxide, tungsten carbide or alumina, giving around 70–72 HRc, then ground to finish. This is the area most prone to fretting.

Stuffing boxes and gland packing

A stuffing box is the chamber sealing the shaft where it passes through the casing. It holds 4–6 packing rings and a gland (end plate) that squeezes them down onto the shaft. The narrow annulus between shaft and packing is a restrictive path: it produces the pressure drop that prevents leakage, and considerable friction that heats the shaft.

Operating practice

Tighten the gland just enough to allow a slight, deliberate leak. That leakage is the packing's lubricant and coolant. A dry-tightened gland runs hot and wears the shaft or sleeve rapidly. In a properly operating stuffing box, friction losses run about 1% of total pump power, independent of pump size and type.

Obviously this cannot be allowed on hazardous or toxic liquids — which is precisely why gland packing is not used on those services.

Stuffing box variants

  • Lantern ring — a ring drilled circumferentially that replaces one packing ring, positioned at the pump end or midway through the packing set. Used for dirty or high-pressure liquid, it admits clean sealing liquid into the packing set.
  • Throat (restrictive) bush — fitted at the stuffing box throat where discharge pressure is high.
  • Water jacket — integrally cast into the stuffing box for liquids above about 120 °C, allowing cooling water circulation to keep the packing temperature down.
  • Repeller (auxiliary impeller) — radial blades on the back shroud of an auxiliary impeller that throw liquid outward and so reduce the pressure in front of the packing. Used for toxic or corrosive liquids where complete sealing is necessary. The pressure it generates depends on blade length and casing clearance.
  • Back vanes — cast on the main impeller itself, serving the same pressure-relief function.

Work done against packing friction is influenced by packing quality and type, gland length, shaft diameter, rotational speed, pressure acting on the gland, and the volume of liquid passing through the packing.

Packing materials

Present-day pump packing is predominantly PTFE (Teflon) / graphite filament, braided and formed into square section, chosen for heat dissipation and low friction. Additional materials are braided in to handle specific conditions — contaminants, acids, alkalis, temperature and speed. Aramid, for example, enhances mechanical properties, resisting packing extrusion and standing up to slurries and abrasives.

Mechanical seals and seal housings

Gland packing has three persistent disadvantages: it leaks continuously and loses product if the shaft surface is not smooth; over-tightening heats the shaft or sleeve and wears it rapidly; and it needs constant supervision. As a result, packing is being phased out except on non-critical, low-power duties. Most applications use mechanical seals.

A mechanical seal comprises two perfectly lapped mating faces, one stationary and one rotating. The crucial conceptual difference from packing: in packing the leakage resistance acts along the shaft axis; in a mechanical seal it acts orthogonal to it.

Seal faces cannot run against each other dry — that causes early wear, and seal damage means leakage. Sealant fluid is injected into the seal housing at a specified pressure to lubricate and cool the faces. Fluid escaping between the faces to atmosphere is fugitive emission; where that is unacceptable, multiple seal arrangements are used. Seals are precision devices and demand corresponding care during pump assembly.

The three points of sealing

Every mechanical seal installation has three:

  1. At the mating surfaces of the primary and mating rings — the primary sealing face.
  2. Between the rotating component and the shaft or sleeve — a secondary seal.
  3. Between the stationary component and the gland plate — a secondary seal.

A seal mounted on a sleeve adds a fourth point between shaft and sleeve, and some mating ring designs add a seal between gland plate and stuffing box.

Mating faces are normally dissimilar materials, held in contact by a spring whose preload keeps the primary and mating rings together during shutdown or whenever liquid pressure is absent.

Why the secondary seal must be dynamic

The secondary seal between shaft and sleeve must be partially dynamic. As the faces wear, the primary ring has to creep forward. Machine vibration, shaft runout and differential thermal expansion between shaft and casing all require the secondary seal to move along the shaft. That flexibility comes from the secondary seal form — O-ring, wedge, V-ring or bellows. Most designs fix the seal head to the sleeve or shaft and provide positive drive to the primary ring.

Seal arrangements

  • Single seal — internally or externally mounted. A single seal mounted inside the seal chamber represents at least 75% of all installations and is the most economical sealing system available.
  • Dual seal — tandem seals, or double seals (externally or internally pressurised).

Seal housings

Seals are housed in seal housings the way packing is housed in stuffing boxes. Research shows an enlarged bore confers distinct advantages: fluid flow within the enlarged chamber increases, which aids removal of seal-generated heat. Properly selected enlarged chambers help deal with:

  • gas in the seal housing under start/stop conditions;
  • light hydrocarbons with low boiling points;
  • liquids carrying solid particles.

Bearing housings and isolators

Overhung (cantilever) impeller pumps

These normally use anti-friction bearings only. The radial ball or cylindrical roller bearing sits inboard, adjacent to the impeller, arranged to take radial load only. The thrust bearing sits closest to the coupling — usually a duplex pair of angular contact bearings or a double-row angular contact bearing.

  • Ball bearing inboard → outboard is a duplex angular contact pair face to face, or a double-row angular contact bearing.
  • Cylindrical roller bearing inboard → outboard pair mounted back to back so axial thrust can be carried in either direction. This duplex pair takes both the unbalanced axial thrust and radial load.

Impeller-between-bearings pumps

Radial and thrust bearings have individual housings. The radial bearing is normally at the coupling end; the ball thrust bearing is at the outboard end.

The thrust bearing must be secured axially on the shaft to transmit load through the bearing to the housing — usually located against a shaft shoulder and locked with a bearing nut. That makes the shaft diameter under the thrust bearing smaller than under the radial bearing. Mounting the radial bearing at the inboard (coupling) end therefore gives a larger shaft diameter where torque has to be transmitted from the coupling to the impeller — the shaft under the outboard thrust bearing can be relatively small because no torque passes through it.

The thrust bearing is locked axially in its housing; the radial bearing is left axially loose in its housing to permit thermal growth.

A popular arrangement for in-between-bearing double-suction pumps is journal radial bearings plus a ball thrust bearing. All radial load goes to the journal bearings. The ball thrust bearing is mounted so it carries axial load only, with the housing radially loose around it and a metallic strap on the outer rings locking into the housing to prevent outer-ring rotation. This suits higher horsepower and higher speed duty where ball radial bearings would be impractical on speed, load or lubrication limits.

Vertical pumps

In vertical pumps the thrust bearing carries the unbalanced axial hydraulic force plus the static weight of the rotating element. By design it may sit in the driver or, more usually, at the head end within the pump casing. Bearing types range from ordinary ball, angular contact and split inner race angular contact up to spherical roller thrust bearings on large machines such as vertical deep well pumps.

These bearings are typically rated for at least three times the maximum calculated thrust load, because so many factors are uncertain:

  • thrust calculation is not highly accurate;
  • thrust increases as internal clearances increase;
  • thrust varies with the vertical position of the impellers in the casings;
  • thrust varies with flow, and may even reverse direction.

The most severe thrust loads occur at shutdown and from impact loading caused by water hammer. A reasonable margin between the driver thrust bearing rating and the maximum calculated pump thrust is essential.

Bearing housing protection

Seal life and bearing life are coupled. Liquid leaking from a mechanical seal can fail the bearings; a bearing in poor condition shortens seal life. The blunt reality: only about 10% of rolling element bearings achieve their 3–5 year design life.

Rain, product leakage, debris and wash-down water entering the bearing housing contaminate the lubricant, and the effect is catastrophic:

0.002% water in oil
−48 % bearing life
0.10% water in oil
−90 % bearing life

End seals protect the housing — but in almost every case the end seal's own life and quality is far below that of the bearings it protects. Improving the end seal directly extends bearing life.

Couplings

Pump couplings are generally general-purpose couplings: standardised, less sophisticated, lower cost, with an easily inspected and replaced flexible element and undemanding alignment tolerances. Special-purpose couplings for turbomachinery are covered by API 671.

Coupling types and typical power range
TypeConstructionTypical rating
Gear Two externally toothed hubs engaging internal teeth in a one- or two-piece flanged shroud. Grease packed between the meshing teeth, contained by static seals (O-rings) at the flange faces and shroud. Misalignment is accommodated by sliding between external and internal teeth. Above about 75 kW

Elastomeric shear Driving and driven hubs in separate planes; the elastomeric element is stretched between them, transmitting and cushioning the force. Below about 75 kW. Tolerates the most parallel misalignment of any coupling type.

A useful failure mode

A jaw coupling keeps transmitting torque even if the spider breaks — the driving jaws simply rotate until they contact the driven jaws directly. Operation continues, with considerable noise and accelerated wear, but in some cases that prevents an expensive unplanned shutdown.

Jaw couplings typically accommodate angular misalignment up to 1° and parallel misalignment up to 0.015 in.

Spider configurations

  1. Standard solid centre — the conventional form.
  2. Open centre.
  3. Snap wrap — a flat-strip, open-end design connecting the spider legs around the coupling perimeter rather than at the centre. It can be removed or installed without disturbing either hub's alignment, and because there is no centre connection it does not overlap into the bore, allowing shaft ends to extend at maximum bore diameter to a minimal gap. It must be retained by a ring or a collar: ring-retained is limited to 1750 rpm, whereas the collar configuration achieves the standard coupling's full speed rating because the collar attaches to one hub.
  4. Load cushions — small separate blocks, installed and removed radially, which is very helpful for maintenance on heavy-duty applications. Some models require a collar to hold the cushions in place.
