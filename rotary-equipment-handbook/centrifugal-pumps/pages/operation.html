Operation and Characteristic Curves

Reading and using the four characteristic curves: curve stability and steepness, why the H–Q and P–Q curves take the shape they do, parallel and series operation, speed–torque at start-up, flow regulation, and the limits of the allowable operating region.

A centrifugal pump's most attractive feature is its ability to perform across a wide range of operating conditions — flow rate, suction and discharge head, speed, and liquid type and properties. But it is designed for one discrete combination of flow, head and speed: the Best Efficiency Point. In practice a pump is rarely operated at the parameters it was designed for, so what matters is knowing how it responds when conditions move.

Consider a pump discharging into the bottom of an elevated vessel. As the vessel fills, the differential head the pump must generate rises, and as H rises, Q falls. If power demand rises with it, the load on the motor may pull the driver speed down. Process and utility duties that span a wide flow and head range may demand several pumps running together in one system — where each pump's performance is affected by the others.

The four characteristic curves

Hydraulic properties are studied with shaft speed N held constant. The relationship between differential head and flow is then obtained by throttling the discharge valve: each valve position gives a flow rate and a corresponding head. That experiment produces the fundamental characteristic of any centrifugal pump:

H = f(Q)  ·  P = f(Q)  ·  η = f(Q)  ·  NPSH-r = f(Q)

Flow Q on the abscissa (m³/h, m³/s, l/s, l/min, US gpm, Imp gpm); head H (m, ft), power P (kW, hp) and efficiency η (% or decimal) on the ordinate. The NPSH characteristic is supplementary to the other three.

How specific speed shapes the curves

Specific speed has a strong influence on the character of all four curves. The shapes look different between a low-Ns radial pump and a high-Ns propeller pump, but the trends are consistent and worth committing to memory.

Curve character against specific speed
CurveLow Ns (radial)Medium Ns (mixed flow)High Ns (axial)
H–Q Gently drooping — relatively flat Steeper Steepest, with a pronounced slope toward shut-off and beyond BEP
P–Q Rises with flow → overloading Approximately flat Falls as flow rises → non-overloading

Start-up rule and why it exists

AC induction motors should be started with minimum load to limit starting current. For a radial impeller pump the P–Q curve rises with flow, so minimum power is at zero flow — start with the discharge valve closed. For a propeller (axial flow) pump the power curve falls with flow, so minimum power is at full flow — start with the discharge valve fully open.

There is a second reason for axial pumps: the hydraulics become unstable at 70–75% of BEP, and on a closed valve the torque reaches normal value before full speed is obtained. Axial and mixed-flow pump flow is therefore rarely throttled at all.

Curve stability

H–Q curves come in two kinds, and the distinction decides whether the pump can be used in parallel.

Stable H–Q curve

Differential head falls progressively as flow increases. The shut-off head HSO is the maximum head the pump will ever develop. One system head corresponds to exactly one flow.

Unstable H–Q curve

Head rises from HSO to a maximum at some intermediate flow, then falls. The consequence is that for one system head requirement H₁ there are two possible flow rates, Q₁ and Q₂.

What an unstable curve does in service

With a head requirement above HSO, pump flow oscillates between Q₁ and Q₂, giving a modulating flow rate and pipeline vibration. This is not a theoretical concern; it is a recognisable field symptom.

Stability in a single-stage pump can be obtained by:

  • reducing the number of vanes;
  • modifying the vane geometry;
  • reducing the vane outlet angle;
  • on multistage pumps, modifying the diffuser shape and return vanes.
API 610 requirement

API 610 requires that all pumps have stable H–Q curves — head at rated capacity must rise continuously until it reaches shut-off head. For parallel operation of single- or double-stage pumps the head rise from rated capacity to shut-off must be 10–20%. For three or more stages a lower percentage is permitted, because the same percentage would produce excessively high shut-off heads.

Steepness

Steepness is associated with a stable curve and is defined as the percentage rise in head from BEP to shut-off:

Steepness % = (HSO − HBEP) ⁄ HBEP × 100

Alternatively expressed as the ratio ΔH/ΔQ. Small ΔH/ΔQ is a flat characteristic; large ΔH/ΔQ is a steep one.

Steepness is set by the number of vanes and the blade outlet angle. Practical industry combinations run roughly from 3 vanes at 17° through 8 vanes at 28°, with the higher vane counts and angles showing droop. The application decides which is wanted.

Why firewater pumps need flat curves

A firewater pump feeds a header serving hydrants. One or many hydrants may open at once, so the required flow can increase sharply and without warning. As flow rises the pressure from the pump must not drop substantially, because a drop in delivered head directly reduces the height to which water can be thrown. Hence the flat characteristic requirement.

Why the curves take the shape they do

The cause of the H–Q curve: outlet velocity triangle

Consider the velocity vectors at the impeller outlet. Speed is constant, so:

Outlet velocity triangle
U₂Peripheral velocity = π·N·d₂/60. Constant, because N is constant.
β₂Blade outlet angle, between the relative velocity direction and U₂. Constant — it is cast into the impeller.
C₂Absolute velocity
W₂Relative velocity, directed along the vane outlet angle
Cm2Meridional (radial) velocity at impeller discharge = Q / area
Cu2Peripheral component of the absolute velocity — the term that generates head

H = U₂ × Cu2 ⁄ g

Theoretical differential head. U₂ and β₂ are fixed; only Cu2 can change.

When flow changes, the relative velocity W through the impeller passages rises or falls. The velocity triangle changes shape, which changes Cu2. Comparing the triangles directly:

  • Flow reduced → Cu2 increases, Cm2 decreases → head rises.
  • Flow increased → Cu2 decreases → head falls.
The self-regulating property

This is why the H–Q curve droops — and it gives centrifugal pumps their most valuable operational characteristic. If total head rises during operation, the pump automatically reduces its discharge so the impeller can overcome the increased resistance. Conversely, a reduction in delivery-pipe resistance stimulates the pump to increase discharge. No control system is involved; it is inherent in the velocity triangles.

The inlet velocity triangle and off-BEP losses

At the inlet, U₁ = π·N·d₁/60 is constant, Cm1 = Q/area, and W₁ is the relative velocity. When flow changes, the angle of entry β1-en changes relative to the fixed blade inlet angle β₁:

  • Q < QBEP → β1-en < β₁ → flow separation, eddies on the inner surface of the vane;
  • Q = QBEP → β1-en = β₁ → clean, shockless entry;
  • Q > QBEP → β1-en > β₁ → eddies on the outer surface of the vane.

In practice a certain pre-whirl exists, which reduces the mismatch between β1-en and β₁ at off-BEP flows, so real losses are less than a straight-entry assumption predicts. These off-BEP flows also cause disturbance and hydraulic loss in the diffuser ring and volute casing.

Conflicting loss exponents in the literature

Sources describe off-BEP hydraulic losses as proportional to the square of flow in one place and the cube of flow in another. The qualitative point is what matters and is not in dispute: losses grow rapidly and non-linearly as the duty moves away from BEP in either direction. Do not rely on a specific exponent for a calculation.

The cause of the efficiency curve

Hydraulic losses occur at every flow other than BEP and grow as the duty moves away in either direction. The other two loss groups — recirculation losses and mechanical losses — are not greatly affected by flow. So there is one flow at which the sum of losses is minimum, and efficiency is maximum there. That produces the inverted-U efficiency curve.

Non-dimensional characteristics

Given the variety of pump types, sizes, flows, heads, powers and efficiencies, direct comparison between machines is difficult. Non-dimensional characteristics solve it, and they are necessary for transferring a known characteristic from one geometrically similar pump to another.

Individual characteristics

Take the normal operating point — the point the pump is supposed to operate at most of the time — and call its values Qn, Hn, Pn, ηn. Treat each as 100% and express all deviations as percentages of normal. Once the normal values are known for a desired characteristic, the rest of the curve follows. The P–Q comparison across specific speeds earlier in this chapter is exactly this technique.

Universal characteristics

Derived from the similarity of centrifugal pumps:

Head coefficient  ψ = H ⁄ (u₂² ⁄ 2g)

Flow coefficient  φ = Q ⁄ (u₂ · A)

u₂
peripheral velocity at impeller outlet, m/s
A
impeller area = π/4 × d₂²

The universal characteristic ψ = f(φ) lets you compare different quantities of the same type and test a pump using another fluid altogether — air, for instance.

The effect of speed change

Everything above assumed constant speed. With a variable speed drive, the inlet and outlet velocity triangles remain similar and all velocities vary in proportion to speed. Hence the affinity laws:

Q₁ ⁄ Q₂ = N₁ ⁄ N₂  ·  H₁ ⁄ H₂ = (N₁ ⁄ N₂)²  ·  P₁ ⁄ P₂ = (N₁ ⁄ N₂)³

Head goes with u₂², hence with N². Power goes with Q×H, hence with N³. Valid only at constant efficiency; divergence from practice becomes significant beyond ±25% speed change.

Constant specific speed curves

Take a point P on the Q–H curve at normal speed N, and its homologous points P₁ and P₂ at speeds N₁ and N₂. Resolving the affinity relations gives:

Hx = H × (Qx ⁄ Q)²

A parabola through the origin. Every point on it has the same specific speed — substitute the proportional speeds into the Ns equation and the result is a constant.

These are constant specific speed curves. Because efficiency is a direct function of specific speed, every homologous point on one of these parabolas has the same specific speed and the same efficiency. That is the geometric reason iso-efficiency contours on a performance map are parabolic through the origin — and it is the basis for the impeller trim construction later in this chapter.

Multiple pump operation

Two configurations: several pumps discharging into a common delivery pipe (parallel — flows add at a common pressure), or pumps sharing the total head requirement (series — heads add at a common flow).

Addition is never as simple as it looks

Total flow or total head is not a simple addition of individual values — especially where the pumps have dissimilar curves. The actual operating point is always set by where the combined pump curve intersects the system resistance curve.

Parallel operation, similar curves

Construct the combined curve by adding the abscissae (flows) at each head — for two identical pumps, double the flow at each head while holding head constant.

It is tempting to assume that combined curve point is where the pair will operate. It is not. The operating point depends on system resistance. A single pump intersecting the resistance curve delivers Qc1. Start the second pump and the resistance curve — which goes with Q² — turns upward steeply, intersecting the combined curve at Qc2.

The parallel pumping disappointment

Qc1 is nowhere near half of Qc2. Because system resistance rises with the square of flow, adding a second identical pump typically yields far less than double the flow. One documented example: two identical pumps that would each deliver 15.75 l/s produced only 18.5 l/s together instead of the naive 31.5 l/s, because the system pipework resistance dominated.

The remedy is on the system side: size the pipework correctly to keep flow resistance low. If the resistance curve is steep, parallel pumping cannot help much however many pumps you add.

Where the flow must be regulated, it is more efficient to throttle only one pump. Combined efficiency for two pumps in parallel:

η = [H × (Q₁ + Q₂) × 100] ⁄ [K × (P₁ + P₂)]

η
combined efficiency, %
H

Q₁, Q₂

P₁, P₂

Combined NPSH-r for pumps in parallel is that of the most limiting pump — the largest NPSH-r.

Parallel operation, different curves

With two dissimilar curves the combined characteristic has a kink. Until the operating point reaches the shut-off head of the higher-head pump, that pump delivers no flow at all — it sees shut-off conditions for itself. Only beyond that point does it begin contributing.

Starting order matters

With dissimilar curves in parallel, start the pump with the lower shut-off head first, then the higher one. Start them in the wrong order and one pump runs at shut-off, churning and heating, delivering nothing. The same trap appears when curves are very flat and one pump's shut-off head is only slightly lower than the other's.

Parallel operation, flat versus steep curves

Three combinations exist: both flat, both steep, one of each. Comparing the first two at the same differential head, the flow gain from starting the second pump (Qc − Qa) is greater for flat curves than for steep ones. Pumps in parallel are therefore used wherever a flat characteristic (small dH/dQ) is wanted.

Series operation

Construct the combined curve by doubling the head ordinate at each flow, holding flow constant. For dissimilar pumps the method is the same.

At any instant the flow through both pumps must be the same. The total heads of the two pumps are equal only if their individual head–discharge characteristics are identical.

Series operation of single-stage pumps is seldom encountered in practice — multistage centrifugal pumps are used instead.

Non-standard liquids

Viscous liquids

As covered under viscosity corrections, higher viscosity reduces head, capacity and efficiency while increasing power. Use the Hydraulic Institute coefficients to derate the clean-liquid curve.

Liquids with considerable solids

Suspended solids increase hydraulic resistance in proportion to their concentration in the liquid. The consequences:

  • flow rate and head developed are both lower;
  • power required is higher;
  • efficiency is consequently lower.

Where the particles are fine they form a broadly homogeneous mixture and the characteristic begins to resemble that of a higher-viscosity liquid — so viscosity correction thinking transfers across.

Slurry curves must be measured

Q–H curves for mixtures of water and solids — sand, slag, sugar beet, potatoes, fish — can only be determined by experiment. The practical approach is to compare measured curves at various solids percentages against clear water and build a nomogram, which can then predict performance for a particular mixture. There is no general correction chart.

Abnormal operation: the complete characteristic

Normal operation means rotation in the direction matching the impeller's backward-curved vanes, and flow from suction (lower head) to discharge (higher head). But four quadrants exist, and all four are encountered during start, stop or multi-pump operation. Together they form the complete characteristic of the pump.

The four operating quadrants
QuadrantConditionWhen it happens
+N, +Q Normal rotation, normal flow Design operation.
+N, −Q Normal rotation, reverse flow As discharge head rises, flow falls to shut-off. If head continues to rise — possible in parallel operation — liquid flows backward through the running pump. Reverse flow increases as discharge head rises above shut-off.
−N, +Q Reverse rotation, normal flow Common when induction motor phase terminals are swapped. The pump still delivers from suction to discharge, but head and flow are substantially lower because efficiency in reverse rotation is poor. A classic commissioning fault.
−N, −Q Reverse rotation, reverse flow The machine is acting as a hydraulic turbine — the head drop across the impeller converts to mechanical work. Seen when a system has no non-return valve, or a passing one, and the motor trips: liquid flows backward and the impeller spins in reverse.
Runaway in the turbine quadrant

In the −N, −Q quadrant the shaft needs a brake, or the pump can reach very high speeds. This is the failure sequence behind many reverse-rotation incidents: a passing or missing non-return valve plus a motor trip. It is also why the shutdown procedure includes watching for reverse rotation to check the non-return valve.

Speed–torque characteristics

Pumps are driven machines, coupled to electric motors, IC engines, or steam or gas turbines. The prime mover must be rated and matched to the pump to ensure proper start-up — and that means knowing the pump's speed–torque (N–T) or mechanical characteristic.

The N–T characteristic is plotted with torque as a percentage of nominal torque on the ordinate and speed as a percentage of nominal speed on the abscissa. Theoretically it is a parabola from the origin, proportional to speed squared:

T = k·N²

In practice a certain torque is required at zero speed to overcome mechanical losses in bearings, seals and packing and to accelerate the rotor inertia — so the actual curve starts above the origin.

Curve shape depends on pump type and on whether the discharge valve is open or closed at start. Started with the discharge valve closed, a centrifugal pump demands 30–50% of nominal torque after reaching full speed — the exact value a function of specific speed and rotating mass. Mixed-flow pumps have more steeply falling N–Q characteristics than lower specific speed pumps.

Torque from the prime mover

The driver must deliver more torque than the pump demands at every speed from zero to full load. That excess is what accelerates the shaft to rated speed. Because a pump has a slow-rising N–T characteristic, most prime movers meet this requirement comfortably.

T = (30 × P) ⁄ (π × N)

T
full load torque, kN·m
P
power, kW
N
speed, rpm

Torque varies as speed squared, so from full load torque: ×0.563 at 75% speed, ×0.25 at 50%, ×0.063 at 25%.

Accelerating torque

Tacc = (Wk² × ΔN) ⁄ (308 × Δt)

Tacc
average accelerating torque, lb·ft
Wk²
equivalent moment of inertia = W × K², lb·ft² (W = rotor weight in lb, K = radius of gyration in ft)
ΔN
change in speed, rpm
Δt
time interval for acceleration, seconds

FPS units. Wk² for the train is the sum of the individual moments of inertia.

Worked example. A motor of 200 lb·ft² and a pump of 800 lb·ft² give Wk²EQ = 1000 lb·ft². To accelerate to 1800 rpm in 60 s:

Tacc = (1000 × 1800) ⁄ (308 × 60) = 97.4 lb·ft

More accurate method: strip integration

Tacc above is an average. For a real calculation, divide the area between the motor and pump speed–torque curves into vertical strips whose ends approximate straight lines. Each strip is a speed increment taking place over a definite time interval, and the average Tacc is worked out within each band:

t = (Wk² ⁄ 308) × [ ΔN₁⁄T₁ + ΔN₂⁄T₂ + … ]

At any speed, the difference between motor torque and pump torque is the torque available for acceleration — and it varies greatly with speed. Where the two curves intersect, no torque is available for acceleration: the motor then drives the pump at constant speed, delivering exactly the load torque.

A worked case with Wk² of 18.26 lb·ft² (3.26 motor + 15 pump referred to the motor shaft) and nine accelerating-torque strips from 46 lb·ft down to 11 lb·ft gives a total acceleration time of 2.75 seconds — against which the 60-second average method above shows how much the answer depends on the method used.

Discharge regulation

A pump has to adapt to temporary and permanent changes in process demand. That variation is regulation, achieved either at constant speed or by varying speed.

At constant speed: throttling the discharge valve, bypassing flow, changing impeller diameter, adjustable guide vanes, or modifying the impeller. Where several pumps run in one system, regulation may instead be achieved by stopping some of them — common for boiler feed water, cooling tower and similar utility duties.

Throttling

The commonest method, manual or by control valve, and the simplest. But it is confined mostly to radial impeller, low specific speed pumps — as specific speed rises the P–Q curve flattens or droops, and throttling then saves no energy at all.

Throttling is inefficient for four distinct reasons:

  • the increased pressure drop across the closing valve is wasted energy;
  • introducing even a fully open control valve adds a dynamic head loss — typically taken as 10% of the other losses;
  • throttling shifts the system curve and moves the operating point along the Q–H curve away from BEP in either direction, reducing pump efficiency;
  • control valve installations require bypass piping and isolation valves for valve repair.

Use throttling where regulation is temporary or continuously changing. Where permanent regulation is wanted, modify the impeller instead.

Bypass

Divert part of the discharge back to the pump suction or to another system — in effect splitting the pump flow between two systems of different resistance. Normally used on high specific speed pumps and on boiler feed water pumps with large demand variation.

Impeller trim

Trimming the impeller brings a permanent change in flow and head. When outside diameter d₂ is reduced to d₂′, the outlet velocity triangle changes: peripheral velocity U₂ reduces and the outlet angle β₂ increases. Impeller width b₂, vane length and vane overlap all change too.

Volute versus diffuser pumps trim differently

Paired with a volute, impellers can be trimmed along with the shrouds. Paired with a diffuser ring, the gap between impeller and diffuser must not be increased — so trim only the vanes and make no cut on the shrouds. Getting this wrong permanently damages Gap-A.

Graphical construction for the new diameter

Plot the new required operating point B on the Q–H curve. Draw line 0B from the origin through B and extend it to intersect the original Q–H curve at A. Project A and B to read QA, HA, QB, HB, then apply the affinity relations. When A and B are close, constant efficiency can be assumed. For large trims, apply the trim correction factor.

For high specific speed pumps with an inclined outlet edge, the calculation is based on the central streamline — the centreline of the impeller passage. The trimmed outlet edge should pass through point P, the intersection of the original outlet edge and the chord of the inlet edge.

Adjustable guide vanes (pre-whirl)

Some pumps have inlet guide vanes, much like a centrifugal fan. Adjusting them induces pre-whirl in the liquid entering the impeller, which changes flow and head.

  • Positive pre-whirl (liquid rotating with the impeller) is suitable for regulation: increasing pre-whirl reduces flow and discharge head.
  • Negative pre-whirl is unsuitable — it reduces pump efficiency.

Effectiveness depends strongly on specific speed: pre-whirl regulation is not very effective in low specific speed pumps and improves as specific speed rises. Outlet guide vane designs also exist.

Adjustable impeller blades

In propeller pumps, variable-pitch blades change flow and head by altering the blade inclination angle (β + α) without changing diameter. Reducing the angle reduces discharge without an appreciable drop in efficiency, and the change in head is also not substantial.

Speed variation

The most economical method: no throttling losses, and minimal deviation from rated efficiency. The key distinction from throttling is which curve moves — throttling shifts the system resistance curve; speed variation moves the pump Q–H curve along the system resistance curve.

Energy supplied to the pump reduces with speed, whereas throttling and bypass supply full energy even at a reduced operating point. The drawbacks:

  • effectiveness depends heavily on the shape of the pump curve;
  • not all pump designs can be effectively speed-controlled — particularly those requiring recycle control;
  • there is still some loss of pump efficiency, though far less than with throttling.

The allowable operating region

It is almost improbable that a pump in actual service operates at BEP. Since that cannot be arranged in most cases, the selection must at least place the range of operation near BEP. Defining that range matters because outside it you get excessive hydraulic thrust, temperature rise, erosion and separation cavitation — and the consequences are premature bearing and seal failures from shaft deflection, plus process fluid temperature rise in the casing that seizes close-tolerance parts.

Usual allowable region
70–120 % of BEP flow
Axial flow pumps

Operating below BEP

Toward shut-off, a percentage of the process fluid recirculates in the impeller eye and between impeller shroud and back-plate. Evidence of minimum-flow problems is more dramatic where NPSH-a exceeds NPSH-r by two-, three- or fourfold — which is counter-intuitive and worth noting.

The liquid churning in the casing absorbs the pump's inefficiency. Efficiency measures useful conversion of mechanical energy into head and flow; the inefficient part goes into heating the liquid. That leads to vapour formation, expansion of internals and seizure, or exceeding the temperature limits of the materials of construction.

Allowable temperature rise limits

Limit the allowable rise to 50 °F (28 °C) maximum. Use a lower value if 50 °F would exceed the casing design temperature, or raise vapour pressure enough to create a critical NPSH condition, or cause damaging vaporisation at the mechanical seal face. For boiler feed water the rise is limited to 15 °F.

And the maximum allowable temperature is not always set by flashing. A polypropylene pump may have an upper limit of 180 °F where the cavitation-avoidance limit is 210 °F. Mechanical seals, packing, bearings and wear ring tolerances may each dictate a lower ceiling. For non-Newtonian, viscous and shear-sensitive fluids where temperature affects product integrity, the limit may be set by the product.

Minimum flow for mechanical protection

Separately from thermal protection, there is a minimum flow for mechanical protection. With cool clean liquids the thermal minimum may be very small — but excessive shaft deflection from unbalanced radial loads, vibration and rotating element instability will still occur if the mechanical minimum is not met. These scenarios become more evident as suction pressure increases further beyond NPSH-r.

Maintaining minimum flow

  • A permanently open bypass with a fixed orifice in the line.
  • A bypass opened at start-up and closed once the pump is at design capacity.
  • An automatically controlled device sensing process pressure — the most practical and cost-efficient method. As pressure rises to a set point the device opens, diverting bypass flow; as pressure falls it modulates, limiting bypass flow.

Operating above BEP

The problems to the right of BEP are different. For low specific speed pumps the overloading power characteristic means power demand keeps rising, which ultimately trips the motor.

The second problem is breakaway NPSH-r. At higher flow, internal fluid velocities are higher, and by Bernoulli the static head component falls — approaching vapour pressure. The static pressure must therefore be supplied externally, meaning a higher NPSH-r is required at higher flows.

The margin closes from both ends

Even where a pump has adequate NPSH margin at rated flow, at higher flows that margin can be consumed very rapidly — NPSH-a falls with rising friction loss while NPSH-r climbs. Beyond the breakaway point the margin can go negative, and the pump cavitates. This is the mechanism behind the otherwise baffling symptom of a pump that cavitates when NPSH-a is increased.

Flow at 120% of BEP is sometimes called the end of the curve. Motor selection and sizing for low specific speed pumps is often done treating that as the last operating point on the curve — which is the correct conservative practice, since it is the maximum power the motor can be asked to deliver.
