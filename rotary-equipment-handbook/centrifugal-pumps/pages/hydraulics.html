Chapter 03 · Core reference

Pump Hydraulics, Cavitation and NPSH

The quantitative core of pump engineering — head, flow and system resistance, efficiency and power, the characteristic curve and its corrections, specific speed, and the cavitation, recirculation and NPSH behaviour that destroys more impellers than anything else.

A centrifugal pump energises a liquid and delivers whatever flow the system will accept at the head it can generate. Understanding that exchange means being fluent in a small set of liquid properties and pump parameters. This chapter covers them, then builds to the single most consequential topic in pump engineering: suction conditions.

Liquid properties that matter

Specific gravity

The ratio of the liquid's density to the density of water at 4 °C (1.000 kg/l). Dimensionless. Divide density in kg/m³ by 1000, or density in g/mm³ by 1. Below 1.0 the liquid floats on water; above 1.0 it sinks.

Viscosity

A measure of a fluid's resistance to flow — its internal friction. In Newtonian fluids the shear stress causing flow is directly proportional to the shear strain (rate of deformation), and the ratio of the two is constant at a given temperature. That constant is the dynamic (absolute) viscosity, μ, with units of N·s/m². Viscosity of liquids falls rapidly as temperature rises, which is why heating makes a liquid flow more easily.

ν = μ ⁄ ρ

ν
kinematic viscosity, m²/s
μ

ρ

Vapour pressure

The pressure exerted by molecules that have escaped the liquid to form a separate vapour phase above it. For a pure liquid it depends on temperature alone. Vapour pressure is the quantity that makes cavitation possible, so it belongs at the centre of every suction calculation.

Flow and head: the two things that do not change with liquid

The single most useful idea in this chapter

A centrifugal pump is a volumetric machine. At one point of operation it delivers the same volume flow rate and generates the same head in metres of liquid column for any liquid — hydrocarbon, water, anything. What changes with density is the mass flow, the pressure gauge reading and the power.

A 20 m³/h pump moves 20 t/h of water, but only 16 t/h of a hydrocarbon at SG 0.8 — same volume, same metres of head, 80% of the mass and 80% of the power.

Flow is measured in m³/h, l/s or gpm (US or Imperial). Head is expressed in metres (or feet) of liquid column, mlc. The difference between discharge head and suction head is the differential head — what the pump actually produces.

H = (Pd − Ps) × 10 ⁄ ρ

H
differential head, m of liquid column
Pd
discharge pressure, kg/cm²
Ps
suction pressure, kg/cm²
ρ
specific gravity of the liquid (dimensionless)

System resistance

Flow delivered by a centrifugal pump is set by the total head the system imposes on it. That head has exactly two components:

  1. A static component, Hst in metres — independent of flow. If liquid must be raised from one level to another, this is the difference in level, plus any pressure difference between the vessels.
  2. A friction component, Hf in metres — proportional to the square of flow. This is the sum of losses as liquid passes through pipes and equipment such as heat exchangers.

Ht = Hst + k·Q²   where  k = Hf,design ⁄ Qdesign²

Ht
total system head at flow Q, m
Hst
static head, m (flow-independent)
k
system loss coefficient, from one known design point

Working out the resistance: equivalent length method

The whole flow path from suction vessel to discharge vessel is accounted for. Where the path has many fittings — elbows, reducers, valves, orifices — the losses are higher. To make this tractable, each fitting is converted to an equivalent length: the length of straight pipe that would produce the same loss at that flow. Nomograms of equivalent length for valves and fittings are standard; a second nomogram or friction table then converts total equivalent length and flow into head loss.

Always evaluate suction and discharge separately

Suction-side losses must be totalled on their own to establish that NPSH-a exceeds NPSH-r. Only then are both sides combined, with the static lift, to give total system resistance. Treating the system as one lump hides the cavitation risk.

Efficiency and power

Not all the kinetic energy becomes pressure. Pump efficiency accounts for three loss groups, and is the product of three efficiencies:

  1. Hydraulic efficiency — chiefly disk friction (liquid against the impeller shrouds), a function of speed and impeller geometry, plus shock losses from rapid direction changes through impeller and volute.
  2. Volumetric efficiency — recirculation losses at wear rings, interstage bushes and similar leakage paths.
  3. Mechanical efficiency — friction at the seal or gland packing and at the bearings.

The product of the first two is sometimes called internal efficiency. Every pump is designed for one flow and one corresponding head; efficiency is maximum there and that point is the Best Efficiency Point (BEP). Efficiency falls away at every other flow.

PH = (Q × ρ × g × H) ⁄ (3.6 × 10⁶)

PS = PH ⁄ ηp

The characteristic curve

Every manufacturer supplies a characteristic curve. It is four curves on one sheet, all plotted against capacity Q:

  1. Q vs H — capacity against differential head
  2. Q vs η — capacity against efficiency
  3. Q vs P — capacity against shaft power
  4. Q vs NPSH-r — capacity against required suction head

Reading the Q–H curve

The Q–H curve droops continuously from shut-off (no flow) to BEP and beyond. API recommends the curve rise by at least 10% from BEP to shut-off for single-stage, single-pump operation.

Any pump model can be assembled with trimmed impellers — usually not smaller than 20% below the maximum possible diameter. Trimmed-impeller Q–H curves run parallel to and below the maximum-diameter curve within the operating range. A curve sheet covering all available impeller diameters shows efficiency as iso-efficiency contours across the Q–H field.

A small triangle on the curve marks the rated point — the flow and head the manufacturer guarantees. The rated flow is usually 5–10% above the normal operating point, which is the flow the process actually demands most of the time.

Shut-off head is the head at zero flow, with the discharge valve closed and the liquid churning in the casing. Radial-impeller pumps are started this way, but run time in that condition must be minimised — see temperature rise.

Reading the other three

Q vs η is an inverted U: zero at no flow, rising to a maximum at BEP, then dropping. Flows above and below BEP produce higher hydraulic losses and therefore lower efficiency.

Q vs P is obtained by test on cold water. Because volume flow in m³/h, head in metres and efficiency are all independent of the liquid, the test result is valid for every service liquid — the power simply scales by specific gravity.

Q vs NPSH-r is the measured suction head at which throttling the suction produces a 3% drop in differential head at that flow. NPSH-r depends on the service liquid, but cold water is the most damaging of the commonly pumped liquids, so NPSH-r measured on cold water can be safely applied to hydrocarbons and hot water.

Curve corrections

Curves are generated on cold water at one speed and one impeller diameter. Real service changes at least one of those. Factors let you predict performance without retesting.

Affinity laws

Q₂ = Q₁ × (N₂ ⁄ N₁)
H₂ = H₁ × (N₂ ⁄ N₁)²
P₂ = P₁ × (N₂ ⁄ N₁)³

1, 2
initial and new condition
N
pump speed, rpm

The affinity laws are valid only at constant efficiency. Divergence grows and becomes serious beyond about ±25% speed change. Calculator →

The same relationships are applied by analogy to impeller diameter at constant speed — substituting D for N. This is approximate rather than a true similarity law:

Q₂ = Q₁ × (D₂ ⁄ D₁)  ·  H₂ = H₁ × (D₂ ⁄ D₁)²  ·  P₂ = P₁ × (D₂ ⁄ D₁)³

The trim correction

Because efficiency does not in fact stay constant, there is a discrepancy between the calculated trim diameter and the performance achieved — and the error grows with the depth of cut. The empirical correction:

A = 16.2 + 0.838 × C

A
actual required diameter, % of original
C
calculated required diameter, % of original

Example: affinity laws say trim to 84% → actually cut only to 86.6%. Cut less than the arithmetic suggests, then re-test.

KSB's Centrifugal Pump Design gives an alternative approximate relation based on the square-root ratios:

D₂ ⁄ D₁ ≈ √(Q₂ ⁄ Q₁) ≈ √(H₂ ⁄ H₁)

In all cases, proceed with caution — material removed cannot be put back.

Viscosity corrections

Higher viscosity increases hydrodynamic losses through the pump. The consequences are consistent:

  • brake horsepower requirement increases;
  • head generated reduces;
  • capacity reduces at moderate and high viscosities;
  • efficiency decreases.

The effect is more pronounced in smaller pumps, which is why larger pumps are preferred for high-viscosity duty. The Hydraulic Institute viscosity correction chart gives coefficients for flow, head and efficiency:

Qvis = CQ × Qw  ·  Hvis = CH × Hw  ·  ηvis = Cη × ηw

Subscript w = water performance from the curve; vis = corrected viscous performance. Apply correction for fluids above about 2 centipoise.

Specific speed

Specific speed is the number that characterises impeller type uniquely and coherently. It is, by far, the most important single number describing a pump model.

Specific speed describes an impeller, so for multistage pumps only the first impeller is considered. Calculator →

Units — the perennial trap

Specific speed is conventionally quoted in US units: gpm, ft, rpm, because so much empirical work exists on that basis. This knowledge base follows that convention, as does most literature and most vendor data. Always confirm which system a quoted Ns uses.

  • 1 US gpm = 0.2271 m³/h  ·  1 British gpm = 0.2728 m³/h  ·  1 ft = 0.3048 m
  • Ns(US) = 1.63 × Ns(metric)  ·  Ns(metric) = 0.614 × Ns(US)
  • Ns(British) = 1.5 × Ns(metric)

Formally, Ns is the speed at which a pump, sufficiently reduced in size, would deliver 1 gpm at 1 ft of head — a definition of little practical use. What matters is what Ns indexes:

  • Geometric similarity. Pumps of the same Ns but different sizes are geometrically similar, one being a scale factor of the other.
  • Impeller and volute geometry, including the radial-to-axial progression in Chapter 02.
  • Attainable efficiency. Wislicenus plotted Ns against efficiency in 1947 from commercial pump data; Sabini and Fraser refined it in 1986.
  • Curve shape — the H–Q, P–Q and η–Q curves all change character with Ns (see Chapter 05).
  • Performance chart layout. The BEPs of a family of pump models are lined up along the same specific speed, with size factored upward for higher flows and heads.

Specific speed and attainable efficiency

The Sabini and Fraser curves (1986 Pump Users Symposium) assumed:

  • single-stage pumps only;
  • finish and dimensional tolerance within ±1% for vanes and hydraulic passages;
  • wetted surface finish of 2 × 10⁻⁶ per inch of impeller diameter or better;
  • wear ring clearance of 0.0015 in per inch of ring diameter;
  • suction specific speed not exceeding 8500 (US units);
  • discharge recirculation within 80–90%;
  • uniform velocity profile at impeller inlet;
  • clean water at 150 °F or below;
  • efficiency based on maximum impeller diameter;
  • wet-pit efficiencies based on impellers without back rings or balancing holes.

Lobanoff and Ross studied pumps of six stages or fewer at 3560 rpm and found efficiency rising very rapidly to Ns ≈ 2000, holding roughly constant to about 3500, then tapering. The explanation is structural: hydraulic friction and shock losses are a greater share of total head in high-Ns pumps, while mechanical losses barely change across the range and so dominate the power budget at low Ns.

The modelling law

A related index used for very large pumps in hydroelectric work: two geometrically similar pumps working against the same head will have similar flow conditions — the same velocities at all sections — if they run at speeds inversely proportional to their size. Capacity then varies as the square of the size.

Cavitation and recirculation

Classical cavitation

As liquid is drawn into the pump inlet, the pressure acting on it falls. When the reduced pressure approaches the liquid's vapour pressure at that temperature, the liquid vaporises. Those vapour bubbles travel into the impeller, where pressure rises again and they collapse — implode. That implosion is cavitation.

Damage ranges from minor pitting to catastrophic failure, depending on fluid characteristics, energy level and duration. It occurs mostly within the impeller, specifically on the leading face of the non-pressure side of the vanes, where bubbles begin to collapse and release their energy. The signature is a pockmarked, rough surface and severe thinning of the vanes by metal erosion.

Recirculation — a different mechanism, often misdiagnosed

Recirculation is flow reversal at the inlet or outlet tips of the impeller vanes. Cavitation-type damage on the inlet vanes that is not associated with inadequate NPSH can be traced directly to operation in the suction recirculation zone; similar damage at the discharge vane tips to the discharge recirculation zone.

The capacities at which suction and discharge recirculation begin are set by the impeller inlet and outlet design respectively. The casing influences the intensity of discharge recirculation but not its inception. Research shows that when the impeller inlet-to-outlet diameter ratio equals or exceeds 0.5, the suction recirculation capacity is effectively also the discharge recirculation capacity.

How suction recirculation becomes cavitation

Operating left of BEP, eddy currents form at the impeller eye. Flow rate through the pump has not dropped — but the eddies effectively reduce the flow channel area. Same flow through a smaller area means higher velocity; higher velocity means a larger friction pressure drop; and when that drop takes the local pressure below vapour pressure, the pump experiences classical cavitation initiated by recirculation.

A second explanation: as fluid flows over a vane the near-surface pressure falls and flow tends to separate. Separation occurs once the incidence angle — the difference between flow angle and vane inlet angle — exceeds a critical value. The stalled region contains vapour surrounded by turbulent liquid at above vapour pressure; it fills with liquid from the downstream end, the vapour pocket collapses, and the vane surface is damaged. This can happen 200–300 times per second.

Why diagnosis goes wrong

Recirculation produces noise, vibration and pressure pulsations that imitate classical cavitation, so it is routinely misdiagnosed as cavitation — and then "corrected" by raising NPSH-a, which does nothing. The physical tell is location: recirculation damages the opposite side of the vane from classical cavitation.

The fix for recirculation is operating closer to BEP, or a pump designed for lower suction specific speed — not more suction head.

It is often assumed only high-energy pumps are affected. (API 610 6th edition defined high-energy as head above 650 ft / 198 m and more than 300 hp / 224 kW per stage.) But a cast iron or bronze impeller can erode badly at much lower energy levels. Most pumps operate in continuous or intermittent suction or discharge recirculation, especially those designed with high suction specific speed — above roughly 11 000.

Internal recirculation shows up as cavitation noise and erosion damage, rotor oscillation, shaft breakage, or surging, in varying degrees. Much of it is avoidable by designing for lower Nss and limiting operation to capacities above the recirculation point.

Net Positive Suction Head

Two quantities, constantly confused:

  • NPSH-r — Net Positive Suction Head required by the pump to prevent the inception of cavitation and give safe, reliable operation. A property of the pump.
  • NPSH-a — Net Positive Suction Head available from the suction system. A property of the installation.

This field is still, as the literature frankly admits, misunderstood, misapplied and misused — producing either costly over-design of new systems or unreliable operation of existing ones.

NPSH-r: what the number actually means

The Hydraulic Institute defines NPSH-r as the NPSH that causes total head (first-stage head on a multistage pump) to be reduced by 3% due to flow blockage from cavitation vapour in the impeller vanes. It is a practical, repeatable test definition.

The most important caveat in pump engineering

NPSH-r by the 3% definition is not the point at which cavitation starts. That level is incipient cavitation, and the NPSH at incipient cavitation can be 2 to 20 times the 3% NPSH-r value depending on pump design — the higher ratios on high-suction-energy pumps or pumps with large impeller inlet areas.

If NPSH-a merely equals NPSH-r, the pump does not make rated head: it makes 3% less, and it is cavitating.

Suction energy level rises with:

  • casing suction nozzle size;
  • pump speed;
  • suction specific speed;
  • specific gravity of the pumped liquid.

Anything that increases velocity in the impeller eye, the pump's flow rate, or specific gravity increases suction energy. Most standard low-suction-energy pumps can operate with little or no margin above NPSH-r without seriously affecting service life. High-suction-energy pumps cannot.

Theoretical, cavitation-free NPSH-r

A theoretical NPSH-r that genuinely indicates cavitation inception is built from three loss groups: head loss due to friction, head drop due to fluid acceleration, and head loss due to improper fluid entry into the impeller blade.

NPSH-r = K₁·CM1² ⁄ 2g  +  K₂·W₁² ⁄ 2g

K₁·CM1²/2g
friction and acceleration losses — dominant in low suction energy pumps. K₁ is influenced chiefly by the suction nozzle approaching the impeller eye.
K₂·W₁²/2g
blade entry losses — dominant in high suction energy pumps. K₂ is governed by the incidence angle α = (β − θ).
CM1
meridional velocity at blade inlet, m/s = Q ⁄ A
W₁
relative velocity of liquid just before entering the vanes, m/s

In words: NPSH-r is the sum of the losses in the critical region between the suction nozzle and the leading edge of the first-stage impeller blades.

Why worn wear rings raise NPSH-r

QL — the leakage flow — varies with wear: enlarged wear-ring clearances and multistage balance lines both increase it. In lower specific speed pumps leakage is a larger percentage of total flow, so wear has a correspondingly greater impact on NPSH-r. A pump that was fine at commissioning can become NPSH-limited purely through clearance wear.

Why cold water is the worst case

Vapour formation during cavitation or recirculation is a thermal process and depends on liquid properties: pressure, temperature, specific heat and latent heat of vaporisation. For liquid to boil, the latent heat must come out of the liquid flow. The extent of cavitation depends on the proportion of vapour released, how fast it is liberated, and the vapour's specific volume. This is captured in a gas-to-liquid ratio factor Cb (Vlaming, ASME 81-WA/FE-32).

The conclusion: cold water has the greatest potential to cause cavitation damage of the commonly pumped liquids, and cold water is worse than hot water — the specific volume of vapour falls rapidly as pressure and temperature rise. Field experience agrees: pumps on hydrocarbons or hot water operate satisfactorily at lower NPSH-a than cold water demands. Which is convenient, because it means pumps tested on cold water are good enough for those services.

What the research actually says about NPSH

Terry Henshaw compiled the findings of many investigators in Predicting NPSH for Centrifugal Pumps. The practical conclusions:

  • The industry standard defines NPSH-r as NPSH-a with cool water that creates enough cavitation in the impeller eye to drop one-stage head by 3%.
  • Because of the 3% definition, a pump with a larger diameter impeller requires less NPSH than the same pump with a smaller one.
  • NPSH required to achieve 100% head is typically 1.05–2.5× the 3% NPSH-r.
  • NPSH required to suppress all cavitation is typically 4–5× the 3% NPSH-r, though the ratio can range from 2 to 20.
  • "Incipient" cavitation causes minimal impeller damage.
  • The peak erosion rate occurs between the 3% NPSH-r value and the incipient-cavitation value — not at either end.
  • For cool-water service, the 3% head-drop NPSH is not sufficient to prevent cavitation erosion of the impeller.
  • At BEP, NPSH-r does not vary with speed to the exponent 2 — the exponent is more typically 1.5. Consequently Nss at BEP increases as speed increases.
  • NPSH-r curve shape varies with the percentage head drop used. The 3% curve decreases as flow decreases, reaching a minimum at or below 40% of BEP; the 1% and 0% curves increase as flow falls below BEP.
  • Field experience shows pump failure rates rising when Nss (at BEP, US units) exceeds about 10 000, with a pronounced increase at 11 000. HI uses 8500 as the basis for its maximum speed recommendations.
  • At the no-pre-rotation flow rate, NPSH-a for incipient cavitation equals the 3% NPSH-a plus the peripheral velocity head U₁²/2g.
  • To reduce NPSH-r₃ at BEP, impellers are typically designed with Ps1/CM1 > 1, usually about 1.25 — so the no-pre-rotation flow is typically about 25% above BEP flow.

NPSH available

Every pump has an inlet system — vessel, pipes, valves, strainers, fittings. The liquid arrives with some pressure and loses head travelling through that system. The net inlet pressure in absolute terms, after losses, is NPSH-a.

The convention that saves confusion

Strictly, the difference between NPSH-a and NPSH-r must exceed the vapour pressure to avoid vaporisation. By universal convention, the vapour pressure is instead subtracted from NPSH-a — so in pump terminology NPSH-a already includes the vapour pressure correction. Then all you need ensure is NPSH-a > NPSH-r, and the difference is genuinely a net positive suction head.

NPSH margin

The simple approach is the arithmetic difference between available and required. The requirement is that NPSH-a ≥ NPSH-r, and most specifications quote a margin of not less than 1.0 to 1.5 m over the entire range of pump operation. Where the difference is less than the stated margin, the NPSH-r must be established exactly by witnessed NPSH test.

Vertical turbine pumps often operate without NPSH margin and without damage, at slightly reduced discharge head — they are generally low suction energy and cavitation noise is normally not an issue. Even so, NPSH-a must equal or exceed NPSH-r across the allowable operating region including at low water level.

High and very high suction energy pumps operating at the tabulated margins will have acceptable bearing and seal life, but may still suffer impeller erosion and elevated noise.

Additional suction head requirements beyond the table

  • Wear ring clearance growth — increases leakage flow to the impeller eye and disturbs the inlet flow pattern.
  • Gas content in the liquid.
  • Poorly designed inlet piping or casing producing non-uniform suction flow or turbulence.
  • Operation far right of BEP — in that region NPSH-a falls and NPSH-r rises, closing the margin from both ends.

Suction specific speed

Nss = N√Q ⁄ (NPSH-r)3/4

N
pump speed, rpm
Q
capacity at BEP at maximum impeller diameter — halved for a double-suction impeller
NPSH-r
NPSH required at BEP at maximum impeller diameter

US units: Q in US gpm, N in rpm, NPSH-r in feet. Calculator →

Empirically, pump models with Nss below 11 000 operate more stably and prove more reliable. Nss is therefore used as the basis for estimating the safe operating capacity range: the higher the Nss, the narrower the safe band around BEP. Most users prefer Nss in the 8 000–11 000 range.

Pumps with Nss above 11 000 should generally not be operated below 60–70% of BEP flow. Below that they may experience:

  • impeller and casing erosion;
  • shaft deflection and stress;
  • radial and thrust bearing failures;
  • seal problems.

The mechanism is inlet recirculation. Liquid is designed to enter the impeller at a particular angle, correct at BEP. At lower flows it arrives at a very different angle, cannot make a clean entry, and is forced back into the suction pipe, where it recirculates in front of the impeller.

Field evidence of inlet recirculation
  • suction pressure gauge fluctuation;
  • noisy operation;
  • high vibration specifically at low flow rates.

Where a high-Nss model is the only offer, experienced users prefer to buy the slower, more expensive pump instead.

Performance calculation procedure

Field performance calculation has one aim: determine actual pump efficiency, so that deviation from the rated value quantifies performance degradation.

Step 1 — flow

Read from an installed flow device if fitted; otherwise use a non-invasive ultrasonic meter. Flows are often indicated as mass flow; convert to volume flow.

Q = M ⁄ δ

Q
volumetric flow, m³/h
M
mass flow, kg/h
δ
density at pumping temperature, kg/m³

Step 2 — differential head

hs = Ps × 10 ⁄ ρ  ·  hd = Pd × 10 ⁄ ρ  ·  H = hd − hs

Ps, Pd
suction and discharge pressure, kg/cm²
ρ
specific gravity at pumping temperature

Step 3 — hydraulic power

PH = (Q × δ × g × H) ⁄ (3.6 × 10⁶)   g = 9.81 m/s²

Step 4 — motor power at the coupling

Electrical power is fed to the motor terminals, but what matters is power delivered at the coupling — so motor efficiency enters. Motor efficiency is not a fixed number: part-load efficiency is much lower than full-load. Use the manufacturer's efficiency-versus-load table.

PM = (√3 × V × I × cos φ × ηe) ⁄ 1000

PM
motor power at the coupling, kW
V
measured voltage, V
I
measured current, A
cos φ
measured power factor
ηe
motor efficiency at the measured load

Step 5 — pump efficiency

ηp = PH ⁄ PM

Compare against the OEM curve at the same flow. The gap is your degradation — and the input to the overhaul timing calculation.

Non-intrusive alternative

Direct-reading thermodynamic efficiency monitors (the Yates meter and similar) compute operating efficiency dynamically from the temperature rise of the fluid between suction and discharge — measured in millikelvin — together with pressure transducers and a power meter. No dismantling, and the pump can be monitored while running. See Chapter 09.
